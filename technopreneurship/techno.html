1. Entrepreneurship vs Technopreneurship

Entrepreneurship is the process of identifying an opportunity, organizing resources, and accepting the risk of starting and running a business to create value for customers and profit for the founder.
Technopreneurship is a specific form of entrepreneurship in which technology — usually new, emerging, or applied in a novel way — is the core engine of the value created. The technopreneur is not just running a business; the business model itself typically could not exist, or could not scale, without the underlying technology.

The two share the same underlying entrepreneurial DNA: opportunity recognition, risk-taking, resource mobilization, and value creation. What differs is the centrality of technology. A traditional entrepreneur may use technology as a tool (e.g., using a laptop for accounting); a technopreneur builds the venture around a technological capability (e.g., a proprietary algorithm, a hardware device, a platform).

Role of technology: in technopreneurship, technology is the source of competitive advantage — it is what makes imitation hard and scaling possible. Innovation is the mechanism by which that technology is turned into something customers value. The business model then determines how the venture captures part of that value as revenue. All three — technology, innovation, business model — must align for a technopreneurial venture to succeed; a brilliant technology with no viable business model will not survive.

2. Concept of Technopreneurship

Definition: Technopreneurship is the practice of building a business venture whose competitive advantage and growth are driven primarily by the application of technology to solve a real problem, identified through opportunity recognition, and delivered through a scalable business model.

Characteristics of the concept: (1) technology-centric value creation, (2) high innovation orientation — continuous improvement is expected, not optional, (3) opportunity identification — technopreneurs actively scan for unmet or poorly-met needs that technology can address, (4) value creation for multiple stakeholders (customers, investors, society), and (5) willingness to operate under high uncertainty because technology outcomes are less predictable than traditional business outcomes.

3. Traits and Characteristics of a Technopreneur

Vision and opportunity recognition

Technopreneurs notice gaps between what technology can now do and what the market still lacks, and they translate that gap into a concrete venture idea.

Risk tolerance

Because technology outcomes are uncertain, technopreneurs must be comfortable investing time and money into something that might not work, while still managing that risk responsibly (e.g., through small experiments before large investments).

Technical fluency

They need not personally code or engineer everything, but they must understand enough of the underlying technology to make sound decisions and communicate credibly with technical teams and investors.

Adaptability / resilience

Technology projects frequently fail on the first attempt; the ability to pivot, iterate, and persist is essential.

Networking and resource mobilization

Technopreneurs must attract talent, capital, and strategic partners, since most technology ventures cannot be built by one person alone.

Customer-centric problem solving

The best technopreneurs stay anchored to a real customer problem rather than falling in love with the technology itself.

What is Innovation?

Innovation is the successful implementation of a new or significantly improved idea, method, product, or process that creates value. An idea alone is not innovation — innovation requires the idea to be put into practice and to generate value for someone (a customer, a firm, society).

Innovation differs from invention: an invention is the creation of something new (a device, technique, or concept); innovation is the successful commercial or practical application of that invention (or of an existing idea applied in a new context).

Research vs Development, and Translational Research

TermMeaningFocus
ResearchSystematic investigation to generate new knowledge or understanding"Why" and "what is possible" — often not yet product-ready
DevelopmentApplying existing knowledge to design, build, and refine a usable product or process"How to build it" — turning knowledge into a working solution
Translational researchThe bridge activity that takes research findings and actively works to convert them into practical, deployable applicationsClosing the gap between lab-stage knowledge and market-ready technology

Types of Innovation

TypeDefinitionExample
Product innovationA new or significantly improved good or service, in terms of its features or capabilitiesA smartwatch that adds continuous glucose monitoring
Process innovationA new or improved way of producing or delivering an existing product/service, usually improving cost, speed, or qualitySwitching from manual quality checks to automated visual inspection on a production line
Business model innovationA new way of creating, delivering, and capturing value — even if the underlying product is unchangedMoving a software product from one-time purchase to a subscription (SaaS) model

These types are not mutually exclusive: a single venture can combine all three, e.g., a new sensor (product), an automated calibration process (process), and a subscription-based data service around it (business model).

Innovation-driven Venture vs Small-Medium Enterprise (SME)

An innovation-driven venture pursues rapid, scalable growth based on a novel product, technology, or business model, typically seeking outside investment to grow fast. A small-medium enterprise (SME) typically serves a local or limited market, grows more gradually, and may rely on proven, established business practices rather than novel technology.
Common mistake: assuming every SME is "non-innovative" or that innovation-driven ventures cannot be small. Many SMEs innovate steadily within their niche, and innovation-driven ventures often start as small teams. The real distinction is growth ambition and reliance on novelty for competitive advantage, not company size.

Worked example — classifying an idea

Scenario: A university robotics lab, skilled in low-cost sensor design, decides to build an affordable air-quality monitor after noticing that many households in polluted cities cannot afford imported monitors.
Step 1 — identify the origin: The starting capability (sensor design expertise) is organization-driven; the specific target problem (affordability for households) is market-driven.
Step 2 — classify the innovation type: A new physical device = product innovation; if they also introduce a low-cost local assembly process, that adds process innovation.
Conclusion: This is a hybrid case — capability-led origin, market-validated direction — which is common in strong technopreneurial ventures.

Feature vs Benefit

A feature is a factual attribute of a product ("what it has"). A benefit is what that attribute actually does for the customer ("what it means for them"). Customers buy benefits, not features — features only matter to the extent they produce a benefit connected to a real need.
FeatureBenefitUnderlying customer need
Battery lasts 30 daysDo not need to worry about chargingConvenience, reliability
App uses on-device AI (no internet needed)Works in rural areas with poor connectivityAccessibility for underserved regions
Automated invoice generationSaves the shop owner 5 hours per weekTime savings, reduced admin burden

High Value-Adding, Solution-Driven, and Efficiency-Improvement Value

A value proposition is high value-adding when it produces a large improvement relative to current alternatives. Value typically comes from one of two directions: (1) solution-driven value — solving a problem the customer previously could not solve at all, and (2) efficiency-improvement value — doing something the customer could already do, but faster, cheaper, or with less effort.

The Value Formula

Value = Benefits ÷ Cost

Value increases when benefits rise relative to cost, or when cost falls relative to benefits. This is why both "add more benefit" and "reduce cost/effort" are legitimate innovation strategies.

Worked example 1 — basic value comparison

Problem: A small clinic currently uses a manual filing system costing effectively 10 "benefit units" of time-savings potential at a cost of 8 units (mostly staff time). A new digital records system offers 18 benefit units at a cost of 6 units.
Given: Manual — Benefits=10, Cost=8. Digital — Benefits=18, Cost=6.
Formula: Value = Benefits/Cost.
Calculation: Manual value = 10/8 = 1.25. Digital value = 18/6 = 3.0.
Answer: The digital system delivers roughly 2.4× the value of the manual system.
Interpretation: Even though "benefit units" are illustrative, the exercise shows that value rises both because benefits went up and cost went down — a doubly strong value proposition.

Worked example 2 — effect of raising price (cost)

Problem: A SaaS tool offers 20 benefit units. Its price (cost) is currently $40/month, giving Value = 20/40 = 0.5. Management considers raising the price to $60/month without changing the product.
Calculation: New value = 20/60 = 0.33.
Interpretation: Raising price without adding benefit reduces perceived value; to preserve or increase value at $60, the firm must also increase benefits (e.g., add a feature) so that Benefits/60 ≥ 0.5, meaning Benefits must rise to at least 30.

The Value Proposition Statement

A complete value proposition statement is built from four elements: (1) Needs — the specific customer need being addressed; (2) Approach — the solution/product delivering it; (3) Benefits per cost — the value ratio, stated concretely; (4) Competition — how this compares with current alternatives.
Example value proposition statement

"For small poultry farmers who need to detect disease outbreaks early (Need), our low-cost sensor kit with a mobile alert app (Approach) detects early warning signs at 1/10th the cost and in half the time of a veterinary visit (Benefits per cost), unlike current practice which relies on waiting for visible symptoms or expensive lab testing (Competition)."

Customer Needs and Pain Points

A need is a underlying goal or requirement the customer wants satisfied. A pain point is a specific, felt frustration caused by that need being unmet — pain points are the visible symptom of a deeper need.

Example: A shop owner's need is "manage inventory accurately." Her pain point is "I keep running out of stock unexpectedly and losing sales."

Demographics

Demographics are measurable population characteristics — age, gender, income, occupation, location, education — used to describe and segment potential customers. Demographics alone rarely explain why someone buys; they are best combined with behavioral and need-based data.

Market Research and Validation

AspectExplanation
PurposeTo confirm that a real, sufficiently large need exists and that customers will actually act (pay, switch, adopt) to solve it
Primary researchData collected directly by the venture (interviews, surveys, prototype testing)
Secondary researchExisting data from other sources (industry reports, published statistics)
ValidationTesting assumptions against real customer behavior rather than opinions — e.g., a customer saying "yes I'd use that" is weak validation; a customer pre-ordering or using a prototype is strong validation
Common mistake: treating a survey where people say they "like the idea" as proof of demand. Real validation requires observing a costly action (payment, time commitment, behavior change), not just a stated opinion.

Customer Decision-Making Process

Stages: (1) recognize a need or problem, (2) search for information/options, (3) evaluate alternatives against criteria (price, benefit, trust), (4) decide and purchase, (5) post-purchase evaluation, which drives repeat use or churn. Technopreneurs should design outreach and product messaging around all five stages, not just the "decide" stage.

Target Customer Profile

A target customer profile identifies: who the customer is (demographics/firmographics), what specific need/pain point they have, what triggers them to look for a solution, and what behaviors (channels used, budget available, decision authority) shape how they buy.

Persona

Example persona

Name: Anita, 34 — Role: Owner of a small retail electronics shop — Goals: Reduce stock-outs, save time on manual bookkeeping — Pain points: Loses sales due to unexpected stock-outs; spends 6 hours/week on manual ledgers — Behavior: Uses a basic smartphone, prefers simple apps, price-sensitive — Decision trigger: A recent stock-out that caused a lost sale to a competitor.

Personas are useful because they translate abstract market data into a concrete, memorable "person" the whole team can design for, reducing the risk of building features nobody specific actually needs.

Classes of Competitors

ClassDefinitionExample

Substitute/alternativeThe customer's current fallback, including doing nothingFarmers relying on guesswork or waiting to see if the crop recovers

Product Differentiation

Differentiation is the set of meaningful ways a product distinguishes itself from alternatives in the customer's mind — through features, quality, price, service, brand, or user experience — creating a reason to prefer it over substitutes.

Differentiation supports competitive advantage only when it is (1) valued by the customer, (2) difficult for competitors to copy quickly, and (3) sustainable as the market evolves.

Positioning

Positioning is how a product is perceived relative to competitors along dimensions customers care about (e.g., price vs quality, simplicity vs power). Example: a low-cost, simple mobile app positioned as "the affordable option for smallholder farmers," versus a premium competitor positioned as "the professional-grade solution for large agribusiness."

Market Structures

Perfect competitionManyNone (price takers)Low

Most early-stage technology ventures compete under monopolistic competition — many players, but each tries to differentiate — until a dominant player consolidates the market into an oligopoly.

Market Segmentation

A market segment is a group of customers who share similar needs and respond similarly to a given value proposition. Common segmentation bases: demographic, geographic, behavioral, need-based/psychographic. Segmentation allows a venture to focus resources on the segment where its value proposition is strongest, rather than trying to serve everyone.

Market Size

ConceptMeaning
Total/Potential marketEveryone who could conceivably need the solution
Target market (TAM subset)The portion the venture realistically intends to serve given its positioning
Reachable/served marketThe portion actually reachable given current distribution, budget, and capacity
Worked example — estimating market size

Problem: A country has 2,000,000 smallholder farmers (potential market). The venture's product only suits farmers growing a specific crop, who make up 15% of that group (target market). Of those, the venture can currently reach farmers in regions covered by its distribution partners, about 20% of the target market (reachable market).
Calculation: Target market = 2,000,000 × 0.15 = 300,000. Reachable market = 300,000 × 0.20 = 60,000 farmers.
Interpretation: The realistic near-term opportunity is 60,000 potential customers, not 2,000,000 — this reachable figure should guide near-term planning, while the larger target market informs longer-term growth potential.

Beachhead Market

A beachhead market is a small, specific, well-defined initial market segment chosen because the venture can realistically win a dominant position there, before expanding to adjacent segments.

Characteristics: reachable, has an urgent unmet need, is large enough to sustain the venture initially but small enough to dominate quickly, and provides a credible base ("reference customers") for expanding into adjacent markets. Example: rather than targeting "all farmers," a venture first focuses only on smallholder rice farmers in one region with a known pest problem.

Creating Your Market

Sometimes no existing market segment perfectly matches the venture's value proposition — the venture must create the category by educating customers about a need they had not previously framed as solvable, positioning the offering as the definitive answer to that newly-framed need, and building initial demand before competitors recognize the opportunity.

Important note: Intellectual-property rules, registration procedures, and enforceability vary significantly by jurisdiction (country/region). The explanations below describe general, widely-shared principles for exam purposes; they are not legal advice, and a technopreneur should always confirm specific rules with a qualified professional in their own jurisdiction.

Intellectual Property (IP) — Definition and Importance

Intellectual property refers to creations of the mind — inventions, designs, brand identifiers, written/artistic works, and confidential business information — that the law allows a creator or owner to control and benefit from for a period of time.

Why protect IP: it prevents competitors from freely copying a venture's innovation, preserves the venture's competitive advantage long enough to earn a return on its R&D investment, and can itself become a valuable, sellable, or licensable asset. Cost of protection: IP protection is not free — filing fees, legal fees, and time to obtain protection (especially patents) can be significant, and enforcement (suing an infringer) is often even more costly than obtaining the right in the first place. Technopreneurs must weigh protection cost against the commercial value at stake.

ProtectionWhat it protectsTypical purposeExample
CopyrightOriginal creative/expressive works (software code, writing, art, music)Prevents unauthorized copying/distribution of the expressionThe source code of a mobile app

Contracts, NDAs, and Non-Compete Agreements

Contracts are legally binding agreements that define obligations between parties (e.g., co-founders, employees, suppliers) and are one of the most practical ways to protect business interests, since they can explicitly assign IP ownership and confidentiality duties.

Non-disclosure agreement (NDA): a contract obligating a party (e.g., an employee, contractor, or potential investor) not to disclose specified confidential information. Purpose: protects trade secrets and unpatented ideas during discussions. Limitation: an NDA only binds the signing party — it does not stop independent discovery by a third party, and enforcement can be difficult if a breach is hard to prove.

Non-compete agreement: a contract restricting a party (typically a departing employee or founder) from working for a competitor or starting a competing venture for a specified time and geography. Purpose: protects against a former insider immediately using inside knowledge to compete. Limitation: enforceability and allowed scope (duration, geography) vary greatly by jurisdiction, and overly broad non-competes are often unenforceable.

Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)

CAC = Total sales & marketing cost ÷ Number of new customers acquired
Worked example — CAC

Problem: In one quarter, a venture spends $8,000 on sales & marketing and acquires 200 new customers.
Formula: CAC = Total cost ÷ New customers.
Calculation: CAC = 8,000 ÷ 200 = $40.
Answer: CAC = $40 per new customer.
Interpretation: Each new customer costs the venture $40 to acquire; this must be compared against how much value that customer brings over time (CLV).

Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)

CLV = Average revenue per customer per period × Average customer lifespan (in periods)
Worked example — CLV and CAC vs CLV

Problem: A customer pays $10/month on average and typically stays subscribed for 15 months.
Formula: CLV = Revenue per period × Lifespan.
Calculation: CLV = 10 × 15 = $150.
Comparing to CAC: With CAC = $40 (from above), CLV/CAC = 150/40 = 3.75.
Interpretation: Because CLV substantially exceeds CAC, each customer is profitable over their lifetime; a healthy technology venture generally wants CLV meaningfully greater than CAC, though the "safe" ratio depends on margins, cash-flow timing, and industry — there is no single universal rule.

Cash Flow Statement

A cash flow statement records the actual cash moving into and out of the venture over a period, showing whether the venture has enough cash on hand to operate — distinct from profit, which can include non-cash items.
Worked example — cash flow statement

Problem: In Month 1, a venture starts with $5,000 cash. It receives $3,000 in customer payments (inflow) and pays $2,000 in rent/salaries and $500 in supplier bills (outflows).
Step 1 — Net cash flow: Inflow − Outflow = 3,000 − (2,000+500) = $500.
Step 2 — Closing cash: Opening cash + Net cash flow = 5,000 + 500 = $5,500.
Answer: Closing cash position = $5,500.

Each period's closing cash becomes the next period's opening cash. A projection lets a venture spot upcoming cash shortfalls (e.g., Month 3 shows negative net cash flow) before they actually occur.

Income / Profit & Loss (P&L) Statement

Worked example — P&L

Problem: Monthly revenue = $10,000. Cost of goods sold = $4,000. Operating expenses = $3,500.
Step 1 — Gross profit: Revenue − COGS = 10,000 − 4,000 = $6,000.
Step 2 — Net profit: Gross profit − Operating expenses = 6,000 − 3,500 = $2,500.
Answer: Net profit = $2,500 for the month.

Accrual Accounting vs Cash Basis

BasisWhen revenue/expense is recorded
Cash basisWhen cash actually changes hands
Accrual basisWhen revenue is earned or expense is incurred, regardless of when cash moves

Example: a venture delivers a service in March but is paid in April. Under cash basis, revenue is recorded in April; under accrual basis, revenue is recorded in March, matching it to when the work was actually done. Timing matters because accrual gives a more accurate picture of performance in the period the work occurred, while cash basis better reflects actual liquidity.

Depreciation

Annual Depreciation = (Cost − Residual Value) ÷ Useful Life
Worked example — straight-line depreciation

Problem: A venture buys equipment for $12,000, expected to have a residual (salvage) value of $2,000 after a useful life of 5 years.
Given: Cost=12,000; Residual=2,000; Useful life=5.
Formula: Annual depreciation = (Cost − Residual)/Life.
Calculation: (12,000 − 2,000)/5 = 10,000/5 = $2,000/year.
Answer: $2,000 depreciation expense per year for 5 years.
Note: This assumes straight-line depreciation, i.e., equal expense each year — other methods exist but are beyond this course's scope.

Operating Expenses

Operating expenses are the routine costs of running the business, not tied to producing a specific unit — e.g., rent, salaries, utilities, marketing, insurance — as distinct from cost of goods sold, which scales directly with units produced/sold.

Balance Sheet, Equity, and Liability

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
TermMeaning
AssetsWhat the venture owns (cash, equipment, receivables)
LiabilitiesWhat the venture owes to others (loans, unpaid bills)
EquityThe owners' residual claim — Assets minus Liabilities
Worked example — balance sheet

Problem: A venture has $8,000 cash, $4,000 in equipment, and $2,000 owed by customers (receivables) as assets. It owes $3,000 on a loan (liability).
Step 1 — Total assets: 8,000 + 4,000 + 2,000 = $14,000.
Step 2 — Equity: Assets − Liabilities = 14,000 − 3,000 = $11,000.
Check: Liabilities + Equity = 3,000 + 11,000 = $14,000 = Assets. ✔ Balanced.

Breakeven Time

The breakeven point is the sales volume (or revenue) at which total revenue equals total costs (profit = 0). Breakeven time is how long it takes to reach that point from the start of operations.
Breakeven units = Fixed Costs ÷ (Price per unit − Variable cost per unit)
Worked example — breakeven

Problem: Fixed costs = $6,000/month. Price per unit = $50. Variable cost per unit = $30.
Step 1 — Contribution margin per unit: Price − Variable cost = 50 − 30 = $20.
Step 2 — Breakeven units: Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin = 6,000 ÷ 20 = 300 units/month.
Answer: The venture must sell 300 units per month to break even.
Breakeven time: if the venture currently sells 150 units/month and is growing by 30 units each month, breakeven is reached when cumulative capacity reaches 300 — i.e., in 5 months at that growth rate (150→180→210→240→270→300).

Minimum Viable Product (MVP)

What an MVP is NOT: it is not a low-quality or "broken" product, not necessarily the final product's smallest feature set stripped randomly, and not a substitute for having a real value proposition — it must still deliver the core benefit, just without every planned feature.

Example: before building a full mobile app, a team might test demand with a simple WhatsApp-based service where farmers send photos and get a manual diagnosis reply — testing the core value ("fast diagnosis") without building any app at all.

Iterative design repeats this cycle continuously, so each version of the product is informed by evidence from the previous version rather than by assumption alone.

Time Value of Money

Money available today is generally worth more than the same amount available in the future, because today's money can be invested to earn a return, and because of inflation and risk over time.
FV = PV × (1 + r)ⁿ

Where PV = present value (amount today), FV = future value, r = interest/growth rate per period, n = number of periods.

Worked example — time value of money

Problem: An investor could put $10,000 into a venture today, which is expected to grow at 8% per year. What would that be worth in 3 years?
Given: PV=10,000; r=0.08; n=3.
Formula: FV = PV(1+r)ⁿ.
Calculation: FV = 10,000 × (1.08)³ = 10,000 × 1.259712 = $12,597.12.
Answer: The $10,000 today is equivalent to roughly $12,597 in 3 years at an 8% growth rate.
Interpretation: This is why investors compare an investment's expected future return to what the same money could earn elsewhere.

Price Structure

Pricing should reflect: (1) production/delivery cost (a floor below which the venture loses money), (2) the value delivered to the customer (the ceiling of what they would rationally pay, per the Value = Benefits/Cost concept in Topic 2), and (3) customer willingness to pay, which can differ across segments and often justifies tiered pricing.

Price Elasticity of Demand

Price Elasticity of Demand = (% Change in Quantity Demanded) ÷ (% Change in Price)
Worked example — price elasticity

Problem: A venture raises its subscription price from $20 to $22 (a 10% increase) and observes that monthly sign-ups fall from 1,000 to 900 (a 10% decrease).
Calculation: Elasticity = (−10%) ÷ (10%) = −1.0.
Interpretation: A value with magnitude around 1 indicates demand changed proportionally to the price change ("unit elastic"). If the magnitude were much greater than 1, demand would be highly sensitive to price (elastic); if much less than 1, demand would be relatively insensitive (inelastic). The negative sign simply reflects that price and quantity typically move in opposite directions.

Meaning of a Business Plan

A business plan is a structured written document describing what the venture will do, how it will create and capture value, and how it will be resourced and executed, used both internally (as an execution guide) and externally (to raise funding or attract partners).

Importance of a Business Plan

Criteria of a Good Business Plan

  • Realistic assumptions — figures are defensible, not wishful
  • Logical structure — each section follows from the last
  • Market understanding — demonstrates real customer/market knowledge, not guesswork

Ethics in Technopreneurship

Ethics is the set of principles guiding what is right or wrong conduct. In technopreneurship, ethics governs how a venture treats customers, employees, partners, and society while pursuing profit and growth.

Codes of Ethics

A code of ethics is a formal, written set of principles an organization commits to follow (e.g., honesty in marketing, fair treatment of employees, responsible data use). Purpose: it sets clear expectations internally and signals trustworthiness externally to customers, partners, and investors.

Ethical Frameworks

FrameworkCore ideaApplied example
ConsequentialistAn action's rightness is judged by its outcomes/consequences (e.g., overall benefit vs harm)Deciding whether to launch a feature by weighing its overall benefit to users against potential harms
Duty-based (deontological)Certain actions are right or wrong based on rules/duties, regardless of outcomeRefusing to sell user data even if it would be profitable, because doing so violates a duty of honesty/privacy
Virtue ethicsFocuses on the character and virtues (honesty, fairness, courage) of the decision-maker rather than rules or outcomes aloneA founder asking "what would a person of good character do here?" when facing a pricing dilemma

Broader Ethical Considerations in Technology

  • Privacy — how customer data is collected, stored, and used
  • Fairness — avoiding biased algorithms or exclusionary pricing/access
  • Transparency — being honest about how a product works and its limitations
  • Responsible technology use — anticipating misuse of the technology
  • Stakeholder impact — considering effects on employees, communities, and the environment, not just shareholders

Social Businesses

A social business is a venture whose primary purpose is to achieve a defined social or environmental goal, using a sustainable business model, rather than maximizing profit for owners as the primary objective.

Distinction from conventional ventures: a conventional profit-oriented venture treats social benefit as a byproduct (if it occurs at all); a social business treats social benefit as the core mission and profit as the means to sustain that mission. Example: a venture providing affordable clean-water sensors to rural communities, reinvesting most profit into expanding access rather than maximizing shareholder returns.

Common mistake: assuming all cultures follow identical business norms or communication styles. Effective global technopreneurship requires researching and respecting local norms in each specific market, not applying a single "one-size-fits-all" approach.

Ethical Issues in Global Operations

Operating across countries/cultures raises additional ethical challenges: differing labor standards and worker treatment expectations, differing data-privacy regulations, differing anti-corruption norms and enforcement, and the risk of applying a double standard (behaving more responsibly in the home market than in markets with weaker regulation). A responsible technopreneur applies consistent ethical standards across all markets, even where local enforcement is weak.

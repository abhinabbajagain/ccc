    1.1 Introduction to AI

    Meaning and basic idea

    Artificial Intelligence is the branch of computer science concerned with building machines and programs that perform tasks which, when performed by humans, are said to require intelligence — reasoning, learning from experience, understanding language, perceiving the environment, planning, and solving problems whose solutions are not known in advance.

    The essential idea is a shift in how a problem is programmed. In conventional programming the programmer already knows the exact procedure and writes it down step by step. In AI the programmer instead supplies:

    • a representation of the problem (states, facts, rules, data),
    • a method of reasoning or search over that representation, and
    • optionally a learning mechanism that improves behaviour with experience,

    and the machine works out the specific answer itself. A route-planning program is not told the route; it is told what a road network is and how to search it.

    Purpose and major goals

    • Replicate intelligent behaviour — make machines reason, decide and act sensibly.
    • Solve computationally hard problems — problems with enormous search spaces where exhaustive checking is impractical, using heuristics and knowledge.
    • Handle incomplete, uncertain and noisy information — real environments are rarely fully known.
    • Automate knowledge work — diagnosis, planning, scheduling, translation, inspection.
    • Understand intelligence itself — AI is also a scientific tool for modelling cognition.
    • Improve with experience — learning rather than reprogramming.

    TWO STANDING VIEWS OF AI

    Strong AI claims a suitably programmed machine would genuinely possess a mind and understanding. Weak AI claims only that machines can act intelligently, whether or not anything is understood internally. Everything in this syllabus is weak-AI engineering: we build systems that behave well, and we test them by their behaviour.

    1.3 Definition of AI

    There is no single agreed sentence. The standard academic treatment (Russell & Norvig) organises definitions along two axes — thought versus behaviour, and human standard versus rational standard — giving four families:

    Human standardRational (ideal) standard
    Thought processes Thinking humanly — building systems whose internal reasoning matches human cognition (cognitive modelling) Thinking rationally — systems that reason using the laws of logic; the "laws of thought" tradition
    Behaviour Acting humanly — a machine is intelligent if its behaviour is indistinguishable from a human's (Turing Test) Acting rationally — the rational-agent view: do the action expected to best achieve the goal given what is known. This is the view the rest of this course adopts.

    DEFINITIONS WORTH MEMORISING

    • General: AI is the study and construction of computer systems that perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence.
    • Agent-based (course definition): AI is the study of rational agents — systems that perceive an environment through sensors and act upon it through actuators so as to maximise the expected achievement of their goals.
    • Engineering: AI is the science of making machines do things that would require intelligence if done by people (Marvin Minsky's formulation).

    COMMON MISTAKES — TOPIC 1

    • Defining AI as "making machines think like humans" only — the rational-agent definition is the one the rest of the course uses; give both.
    • Listing characteristics without explaining any of them. A 7-mark characteristics question needs one or two sentences per characteristic.
    • Confusing AI, machine learning and deep learning. ML is a subset of AI; deep learning (multi-layer neural networks) is a subset of ML.
    • Treating the Turing Test as a definition of intelligence rather than a behavioural test under the "acting humanly" view.
    • Writing application lists with no technique attached — examiners award marks for saying which AI method each application uses.

    1.7 Examination practice — Topic 1

    VSQA — 2 marks

    1. Define Artificial Intelligence.2

    AI is the branch of computer science that builds systems capable of performing tasks normally requiring human intelligence — reasoning, learning, perception, language use and problem solving. In agent terms, it is the study of rational agents that perceive an environment and act to maximise achievement of their goals.

    2. What is a heuristic?2

    A heuristic h(n) is a problem-specific estimate of the cost from node n to the nearest goal. It guides search towards promising nodes so the whole state space need not be explored.

    3. Differentiate AI from conventional programming (any two points). 2

    (i) Conventional programs are given the exact algorithm; AI programs are given a representation and a reasoning/learning method and derive the answer. (ii) Conventional programs need complete, well-formed data and give exact output; AI systems tolerate incomplete or noisy data and often give approximate solutions.

    4. State the Turing Test.2

    A human interrogator converses through a text channel with a machine and a human. If the interrogator cannot reliably tell which is the machine, the machine is said to have passed the test. It is a behavioural criterion under the "acting humanly" view of AI.

    5. Define an agent and a percept.2

    An agent is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators. A percept is the agent's sensory input at a given instant.

    6. What is meant by strong AI and weak AI?2

    Strong AI holds that a suitably programmed machine would genuinely have a mind and understanding. Weak AI holds only that machines can be made to act intelligently, without any claim about inner mental states.

    TOPIC 2Intelligent Agent

    The agent view is the organising framework of the whole course: an agent is what AI builds, an environment is what it acts in, and everything else — search, logic, learning — is machinery inside the agent's decision function.

    2.1 Introduction to intelligent agents

    DEFINITIONS

    Agent: anything that can perceive its environment through sensors and act upon that environment through actuators.

    Intelligent agent: an agent that additionally chooses its actions rationally — it selects, for each percept sequence, the action expected to maximise its performance measure, given the evidence provided by the percepts and whatever built-in knowledge it has. It typically shows autonomy, goal-directed behaviour and some ability to learn.

    Agent function: the abstract mapping f : P* → A from every possible percept sequence to an action. Agent program: the concrete implementation of that mapping running on the agent's hardware.

    Agent–environment interaction

    The agent and the environment form a closed loop. The environment produces a percept; the agent's decision function maps the percept (and possibly the stored percept history and internal state) to an action; the actuator carries the action out; the environment changes; a new percept arrives. Learning agents additionally feed a performance signal back into the decision function so that later iterations of the same loop are better.

    Reading the diagram. The environment is the outer dashed box: everything the agent does not control. Sensors are the only channel by which the environment enters the agent, so anything not sensed is invisible to it. The agent box holds the decision function and any internal state. Actuators are the only channel by which the agent affects the world. Because the loop closes through the environment, an action changes the next percept — which is precisely why agent design is harder than writing a single input–output function.

    Perception and action

    • Perception is the conversion of raw sensor signals into a percept the decision function can use — e.g. camera pixels into "obstacle 2 m ahead".
    • Action is the agent's chosen output; the actuator is the device that carries it out; the effect is the resulting change in the environment.

    PEAS — HOW TO SPECIFY AN AGENT (supplementary / additional knowledge)

    Before designing any agent, state its Performance measure, Environment, Actuators and Sensors. For an automated taxi: performance = safety, legality, speed, comfort, fuel cost; environment = roads, traffic, pedestrians, weather; actuators = steering, accelerator, brake, indicator, horn; sensors = cameras, LIDAR, speedometer, GPS, odometer. PEAS is not an explicit syllabus bullet but is the standard way to write the answer to "give an intelligent agent example".

    The clean one-line distinction: a sensor brings information in; an effector is the body part that changes the world; an actuator is the mechanism that moves the effector; an action is the decision being carried out. Many textbooks use "actuator" loosely to cover effectors too — say so, then give the precise version. That earns the mark rather than losing it.

    2.4 Environments and their properties

    The properties of the environment determine which agent design is appropriate. A fully observable, deterministic, static, discrete, single-agent environment can be handled by a simple table or a classical search; a partially observable, stochastic, dynamic, continuous, multi-agent environment demands state estimation, probability and learning.

    PropertyMeaningExample of each sideDesign consequence
    Fully observable vs partially observable Whether sensors give access to the complete state of the environment at each instant Chess (fully) vs automated taxi driving (partially — other drivers' intentions are unseen) Partial observability forces the agent to keep internal state / belief state
    Deterministic vs stochastic Whether the next state is completely determined by the current state and the action 8-puzzle (deterministic) vs dice game or real driving (stochastic) Stochastic environments need probability and expected-value reasoning
    Episodic vs sequential Whether the experience divides into independent episodes, or current actions affect all future onesDefect classification of parts on a belt (episodic) vs chess or driving (sequential) Sequential environments require planning and lookahead
    Static vs dynamic Whether the environment can change while the agent is deliberating Crossword puzzle (static) vs taxi driving (dynamic); chess with a clock is semi-dynamic — the world does not change but the score does Dynamic environments impose real-time response limits
    Discrete vs continuous Whether states, time and percepts/actions take finitely many distinct values Chess (discrete) vs steering angle and speed in driving (continuous) Continuous spaces need sampling, discretisation or numeric optimisation
    Single-agent vs multi-agent Whether other entities whose behaviour maximises their own performance measure are present; multi-agent is further split into competitive and cooperative Crossword (single) vs chess (competitive multi-agent) vs a fleet of delivery robots (cooperative)Competitive settings need adversarial search (minimax, alpha-beta)
    Known vs unknown (supplementary) Whether the agent knows the rules / transition model of the environment A board game with published rules vs a new video game Unknown environments require learning the model (reinforcement learning)

    2.5 Intelligent agent example — complete specification

    WORKED EXAMPLE 2.1 — AUTOMATED VACUUM-CLEANING AGENT

    Problem

    Specify a vacuum-cleaning agent for a two-location world (squares A and B), showing its agent, sensors, environment, percepts, decision rule, actuators and the resulting action; state whether the agent is rational.

    Given / specification (PEAS)
    • Performance measure: +1 for each square cleaned per time step; small penalty for each movement (to discourage useless motion).
    • Environment: two squares A and B, each either Clean or Dirty; dirt can reappear.
    • Actuators: drive motors (Left, Right) and the suction motor (Suck).
    • Sensors: a location sensor and a dirt sensor.
    Percepts

    Each percept is a pair [location, status], so the possible percepts are [A,Clean] [A,Dirty] [B,Clean] [B,Dirty].

    Decision rule (the agent function, as a table)
    PerceptAction chosenReason
    [A, Dirty]SuckCleaning scores immediately; no movement penalty
    [A, Clean]RightNothing to gain here; the only other square may be dirty
    [B, Dirty]SuckAs above
    [B, Clean]LeftAs above
    Step-by-step run
    tWorld statePerceptActionEffect
    0A: Dirty, B: Dirty, agent in A[A, Dirty]SuckA becomes Clean
    1A: Clean, B: Dirty, agent in A[A, Clean]RightAgent moves to B
    2A: Clean, B: Dirty, agent in B[B, Dirty]SuckB becomes Clean
    3A: Clean, B: Clean, agent in B[B, Clean]LeftAgent moves to A
    Result

    Both squares are clean after three actions. Sensors → percept [A,Dirty] → decision rule → action Suck → actuator (suction motor) → environment changes to A: Clean.

    Explanation

    The agent is rational relative to the stated performance measure and its sensors: at each step it takes the action with the highest expected score given what it can perceive. It is not omniscient — it cannot see whether the other square is dirty before moving there. If dirt never reappeared, the rule would become irrational after everything is clean, because the agent would keep moving and accumulating movement penalties; the fix is to add internal state ("both squares known clean → do nothing"), which turns a simple reflex agent into a model-based reflex agent.

    Exam tip

    Whenever a question says "give an example of an intelligent agent", answer with PEAS + a percept table + one traced run. That structure converts a vague question into a full-mark answer.

    COMMON MISTAKES — TOPIC 2

    • Saying an agent is rational only if it always succeeds. Rationality is about expected performance given the percepts — an unlucky outcome does not make a rational choice irrational.
    • Using "sensor" and "percept" interchangeably: the sensor is the device, the percept is the input it delivers.
    • Calling a wheel an actuator. The wheel is the effector; the motor driving it is the actuator.
    • Labelling chess "dynamic". It is semi-dynamic when a clock is used, static without.
    • Calling a taxi environment "fully observable" — you cannot sense what other drivers intend.
    • Confusing episodic with static; they are independent properties.

    VSQA — 2 marks

    1. Define an intelligent agent.2

    An intelligent agent perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators, choosing for each percept sequence the action expected to maximise its performance measure.

    2. Differentiate between sensor and actuator.2

    The complete history of everything the agent has perceived up to the present moment. The agent function maps percept sequences to actions.

    One in which the next state is completely determined by the current state and the action executed, with no randomness. The 8-puzzle is deterministic; rolling dice is not.

    Autonomy — it decides its own actions without continuous human control. Rationality — it selects the action expected to maximise its performance measure given its percepts.

    TOPIC 3Problem Solving

    A goal-based agent cannot act until the problem has been written down in a form it can search. This chapter is that translation step: from an informal problem to a state space, and from a state space to a solution path.

    3.1 Defining problems as a state space search

    THE VOCABULARY OF STATE-SPACE SEARCH

    • State — a complete description of the situation at one moment, containing everything needed to decide what can happen next.
    • Initial state — the state in which the agent starts.
    • Goal state — a state (or any state satisfying a goal test) that solves the problem.
    • Actions / operators — the legal moves available in a state.
    • Transition (successor function) — Result(s, a), the state reached by performing action a in state s.
    • State space — the set of all states reachable from the initial state by any sequence of actions; drawn as a graph whose nodes are states and edges are actions.
    • Path — a sequence of states connected by actions.
    • Solution — a path from the initial state to a goal state. An optimal solution is one of least path cost.
    • Path cost — the sum of the step costs along a path, written g(n) for the path from the start to node n.

    WORKED EXAMPLE 3.1 — WATER-JUG PROBLEM AS A STATE SPACE

    Problem

    You have a 4-litre jug and a 3-litre jug, neither graduated, and an unlimited water supply. Measure exactly 2 litres in the 4-litre jug. Formulate this as a state-space search and give a solution path.

    Given
    • State representation: an ordered pair (x, y) where x = litres in the 4-litre jug (0 ≤ x ≤ 4) and y = litres in the 3-litre jug (0 ≤ y ≤ 3).
    • Initial state: (0, 0). Goal test: x = 2.

    WORKED EXAMPLE 3.2 — FORMULATING THE 8-PUZZLE

    Problem

    Convert the 8-puzzle into a well-defined search problem and state the size of its state space.

    Given

    A 3 × 3 board with eight numbered tiles and one blank. A move slides a tile adjacent to the blank into the blank position — equivalently, the blank moves.

    Method — the five components
    1. States: a 3 × 3 array giving the position of each of the eight tiles and the blank.
    2. Initial state: any given configuration, e.g. [[2,8,3],[1,6,4],[7,_,5]].
    3. Actions: Up, Down, Left, Right applied to the blank, restricted to moves that keep the blank on the board. In the state above the blank is at the bottom middle, so the legal actions are Up, Left, Right (not Down).
    4. Transition model: swap the blank with the tile in the chosen direction.
    5. Goal test: the configuration equals [[1,2,3],[8,_,4],[7,6,5]] (or whatever target is given).
    6. Path cost: 1 per move, so path cost = number of moves.
    Result

    The 9 squares can be arranged in 9! = 362 880 ways, but the parity of a configuration cannot be changed by legal moves, so exactly half — 9!/2 = 181 440 states — are reachable from any given start.

    Explanation

    Note the abstraction: we ignore how hard it is to physically slide a tile, the colour of the tiles, and everything else. A good formulation keeps exactly the detail needed to determine legal moves and to test the goal.

    Exam tip

    If a question says "formulate the problem", write the five headings as headings. Examiners tick them off one by one.

    3.4 Well-defined problems

    A problem is well-defined when all five components — initial state, actions, transition model, goal test and path cost — are specified precisely and unambiguously. The value of this is mechanical: only then can a general search algorithm be applied without knowing anything about the domain.

    ComponentWhat "precisely specified" requiresWhat goes wrong if it is missing
    Initial stateAn exact, complete descriptionThe search has no root
    ActionsFor every state, the finite set of applicable actions Successors cannot be generated; the search stalls or produces illegal states
    Transition modelA function Result(s, a) with exactly one outcome per pair The state space graph is undefined
    Goal testA decidable predicate on statesThe search never knows when to stop
    Path costA non-negative step cost for every action Optimality becomes meaningless; cost-based algorithms such as A* cannot run

    "Play good chess" is ill-defined; "given this board, find a move sequence leading to checkmate, minimising the number of moves" is well-defined.

    3.5 Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSP)

    CSP COMPONENTS

    A CSP is the triple (X, D, C):

    • Variables X = {X₁ … Xₙ} — the things to be decided.
    • Domains D = {D₁ … Dₙ} — the allowed values of each variable.
    • Constraints C — restrictions on which combinations of values are allowed. A unary constraint involves one variable, a binary constraint two.
    • Assignment — a set of variable/value pairs. It is consistent if it violates no constraint, complete if every variable has a value.
    • Solution — a complete, consistent assignment.

    The path is irrelevant: only the final assignment is judged. That is what distinguishes a CSP from ordinary state-space search.

    WORKED EXAMPLE 3.3 — MAP COLOURING AS A CSP

    Problem

    Colour four regions A, B, C, D using Red, Green and Blue so that no two adjacent regions share a colour. Adjacencies: A–B, A–C, B–C, B–D, C–D.

    Given (CSP formulation)
    • Variables: X = {A, B, C, D}
    • Domains: D(A) = D(B) = D(C) = D(D) = {Red, Green, Blue}
    • Constraints: A ≠ B, A ≠ C, B ≠ C, B ≠ D, C ≠ D
    Method

    Backtracking search: assign variables in order, check consistency after each assignment, and undo (backtrack) whenever a variable has no consistent value left.

    Step-by-step solution
    StepAssignment attemptedConstraint checkOutcome
    1A = Redno constraint yet violatedaccept
    2B = RedA ≠ B violated (Red = Red)reject
    3B = GreenA ≠ B satisfied (Red ≠ Green)accept
    4C = RedA ≠ C violatedreject
    5C = GreenB ≠ C violated (Green = Green)reject
    6C = BlueA ≠ C (Red ≠ Blue) ✓, B ≠ C (Green ≠ Blue) ✓accept
    7D = RedB ≠ D (Green ≠ Red) ✓, C ≠ D (Blue ≠ Red) ✓accept
    Result

    A = Red, B = Green, C = Blue, D = Red. Verification of all five constraints: A≠B (Red/Green ✓), A≠C (Red/Blue ✓), B≠C (Green/Blue ✓), B≠D (Green/Red ✓), C≠D (Blue/Red ✓). The assignment is complete and consistent, so it is a solution.

    Explanation

    A and D are not adjacent, so both can be Red — this is why three colours suffice even though four regions are involved. Notice that consistency was checked immediately after each assignment rather than only at the end; this is what makes backtracking far cheaper than generating all 3⁴ = 81 complete assignments and testing each.

    Exam tip

    Present CSP answers as the triple (variables, domains, constraints), then the trace table showing each rejection and the constraint that caused it, then the verified solution. Rejections earn marks — do not silently skip them.

    SUPPLEMENTARY — MAKING CSP SEARCH FASTER

    • MRV (minimum remaining values): assign next the variable with fewest legal values left — it fails fastest.
    • Degree heuristic: break ties by choosing the variable involved in most constraints on unassigned variables.
    • Least-constraining value: among values, try first the one that rules out fewest choices for neighbours.
    • Forward checking / arc consistency (AC-3): after each assignment, delete now-impossible values from neighbours' domains; if a domain empties, backtrack at once.

    3.6 Game playing

    Game playing is problem solving with an adversary. It is a multi-agent, usually deterministic, fully observable, sequential environment in which two players alternate moves and one player's gain is the other's loss (a zero-sum game).

    ElementDefinitionTic-tac-toe example
    Initial stateThe board position and whose turn it isEmpty 3×3 grid, X to move
    PlayersPlayer(s) — whose turn it is in state sMAX = X, MIN = O
    ActionsLegal moves in state sPlace your mark in any empty cell
    Transition / resultResult(s, a) — the position after the moveBoard with the new mark
    Terminal testTrue when the game is overThree in a line, or board full
    Utility / payoffA numeric value of a terminal state from MAX's point of view+1 win for X, 0 draw, −1 win for O
    Game treeThe tree of all move sequences, alternating MAX and MIN levels Root = empty board; depth = number of plies

    Because MIN is trying to minimise MAX's utility, MAX cannot simply search for a path to a winning terminal state — MIN would never cooperate. MAX must pick the move whose worst reply is best. That reasoning is exactly the minimax procedure, with alpha-beta pruning as its efficient implementation; both are taught in Topic 4.

    3.7 Production systems

    COMPONENTS OF A PRODUCTION SYSTEM

    • Production rules — a rule base of IF condition THEN action rules. The IF part is the antecedent/left-hand side; the THEN part is the consequent/right-hand side.
    • Working memory (database) — the current facts describing the problem state; it changes as rules fire.
    • Rule matching (pattern matcher) — finds all rules whose conditions are satisfied by working memory. The result is the conflict set.
    • Rule selection (conflict resolution) — chooses one rule from the conflict set using the control strategy.
    • Rule execution (firing) — performs the action, updating working memory.
    • Control strategy — the policy governing selection and termination: rule order, specificity (prefer the most specific rule), recency (prefer rules matching the newest facts), refractoriness (do not fire the same rule on the same data twice).

    WORKED EXAMPLE 3.4 — A PRODUCTION SYSTEM FOR ANIMAL IDENTIFICATION

    Problem

    Trace a production system that identifies an animal from observed features.

    Given

    Rule base:

    R1: IF has_hair                       THEN mammal
R2: IF gives_milk                     THEN mammal
R3: IF mammal AND eats_meat           THEN carnivore
R4: IF carnivore AND tawny_colour AND dark_spots   THEN cheetah
R5: IF carnivore AND tawny_colour AND black_stripes THEN tiger

    Initial working memory: {has_hair, eats_meat, tawny_colour, dark_spots}

    Method

    Repeat the recognise–act cycle: match → resolve conflicts (here: lowest rule number first, and do not re-fire a rule on the same data) → fire → update working memory.

    Step-by-step solution
    CycleWorking memoryConflict setRule selected Fact added
    1has_hair, eats_meat, tawny_colour, dark_spots{R1}R1 mammal
    2… + mammal{R3}R3carnivore
    3… + carnivore{R4}R4cheetah
    4… + cheetah{ } — emptynonehalt
    Result

    The system concludes cheetah. R2 never fires because gives_milk is not in working memory; R5 never fires because black_stripes is absent.

    Explanation

    Nothing in the program says "identify a cheetah". The conclusion emerges from repeated matching of independent rules against a growing fact base. This is why production systems are modular — a new animal is added by writing one more rule, with no change to the control machinery. The same recognise–act cycle is the engine of forward chaining (Topic 5) and of expert systems (Topic 7).

    Exam tip

    Always show working memory growing cycle by cycle in a table, and name the conflict resolution strategy you used. A trace without the conflict set loses marks.

    COMMON MISTAKES — TOPIC 3

    • Writing operators without preconditions. "Pour 4 into 3" has two distinct cases depending on whether the water fits — treat them as two operators.
    • Confusing the state space (a graph of states) with the search tree (paths explored, where the same state can appear at several nodes).
    • Forgetting that a CSP solution is an assignment, not a path — no marks for drawing a route.
    • In map colouring, checking constraints only at the end instead of after every assignment.
    • Giving 9! as the 8-puzzle state space instead of 9!/2 reachable states.
    • In production systems, firing a rule whose conditions are only partially satisfied, or omitting the conflict-resolution step entirely.
    • Describing game playing as ordinary search: the opponent chooses alternate moves, so a path to a win is not a plan.

    4.2 Depth First Search (DFS)

    Definition. DFS is an uninformed search that always expands the deepest unexpanded node in the current frontier, following one branch to its end before backtracking.

    Purpose. To find a solution with very little memory, at the cost of possibly exploring long useless branches first.

    Working principle. The frontier is a LIFO stack. The node just generated is the next node expanded. When a branch runs out of successors, the algorithm pops back to the most recent unexplored alternative — this is backtracking.

    DFS(problem)
 1  create node ROOT from problem.INITIAL-STATE
 2  STACK ← empty LIFO stack ;  push ROOT
 3  CLOSED ← empty set
 4  while STACK is not empty do
 5      node ← POP(STACK)
 6      if problem.GOAL-TEST(node.STATE) then return SOLUTION(node)
 7      if node.STATE not in CLOSED then
 8          add node.STATE to CLOSED
 9          for each successor s of node, in REVERSE generation order do
10              push s onto STACK        // reversed so the leftmost child pops first
11  return FAILURE

    DFS dives down the leftmost branch. Notice that C and D remain sitting at the bottom of the stack throughout — DFS only returns to them if the whole B-subtree fails.     Advantages: very low memory; finds a deep solution quickly when one exists on the first branch; simple recursive implementation.
    Disadvantages: not complete on infinite/cyclic spaces; not optimal; can waste time on a very deep fruitless branch.

    4.3 Breadth First Search (BFS)

    Definition. BFS is an uninformed search that expands the shallowest unexpanded node first, so the tree is explored level by level.

    Purpose. To guarantee that the shallowest goal is found — which is the cheapest goal when every step costs the same.

    Working principle. The frontier is a FIFO queue. All nodes at depth k are expanded before any node at depth k+1.

    BFS(problem)
 1  node ← ROOT with problem.INITIAL-STATE
 2  if problem.GOAL-TEST(node.STATE) then return SOLUTION(node)
 3  QUEUE ← empty FIFO queue ;  ENQUEUE(node)
 4  CLOSED ← empty set
 5  while QUEUE is not empty do
 6      node ← DEQUEUE(QUEUE)                 // shallowest node
 7      if problem.GOAL-TEST(node.STATE) then return SOLUTION(node)
 8      add node.STATE to CLOSED
 9      for each successor s of node, in generation order do
10          if s.STATE not in CLOSED and s not already in QUEUE then
11              ENQUEUE(s)
12  return FAILURE

    4.4 Depth-Limited Search (DLS)

    Definition. DLS is depth-first search with a predetermined depth limit ℓ: nodes at depth ℓ are treated as if they had no successors.

    Purpose. To cure DFS's failure on infinite or very deep state spaces by bounding how far it may descend.

    Working principle. Identical to DFS except that expansion stops at depth ℓ. Two distinct failure results must be distinguished: failure (no solution exists within the explored space) and cutoff (no solution was found within the limit, but one may exist deeper).

    DLS(node, problem, limit)
 1  if problem.GOAL-TEST(node.STATE) then return SOLUTION(node)
 2  else if limit = 0 then return CUTOFF
 3  else
 4      cutoff_occurred ← false
 5      for each successor s of node do
 6          result ← DLS(s, problem, limit − 1)
 7          if result = CUTOFF then cutoff_occurred ← true
 8          else if result ≠ FAILURE then return result
 9      if cutoff_occurred then return CUTOFF else return FAILURE

    Explanation

    The two runs show the central trade-off. Choosing ℓ too small (ℓ < d) makes the search incomplete — it reports CUTOFF although a solution exists. Choosing ℓ too large restores DFS's weaknesses. If d is unknown, the standard remedy is iterative deepening: run DLS with ℓ = 0, 1, 2, … until a solution is found. This keeps DFS's O(b·ℓ) memory while gaining BFS's completeness and (with uniform costs) optimality, and the repeated work is minor because the last level dominates the node count.

    Exam tip

    Never write "no solution" for a cutoff. Writing "returns CUTOFF — a solution may exist below the limit" is what earns the mark.

    4.6 Best-First Search

    Definition. Best-first search is a general strategy in which the node chosen for expansion is the one with the best (lowest) value of an evaluation function f(n). It is not a single algorithm but a family; the choice of f defines the member.

    Expand next the node n on the frontier with minimum f(n)
    Choice of f(n)Resulting algorithmCharacter
    f(n) = depth(n), expanded smallest firstBreadth-first searchUninformed
    f(n) = g(n)Uniform-cost searchUninformed, cost-aware
    f(n) = h(n)Greedy best-first searchInformed, goal-pulling
    f(n) = g(n) + h(n)A* searchInformed, cost-aware

    Working principle. The frontier is a priority queue ordered by f. Each cycle removes the minimum-f node, tests it for goalhood, expands it, and inserts its successors with their f values. Because the ordering is by f and not by structure, the search can jump between branches — it is neither depth-first nor breadth-first.

    BEST-FIRST-SEARCH(problem, f)
 1  node ← ROOT with problem.INITIAL-STATE ;  node.f ← f(node)
 2  OPEN ← priority queue ordered by f, containing node
 3  CLOSED ← empty set
 4  while OPEN is not empty do
 5      node ← POP-MIN(OPEN)
 6      if problem.GOAL-TEST(node.STATE) then return SOLUTION(node)
 7      add node.STATE to CLOSED
 8      for each successor s of node do
 9          s.f ← f(s)
10          if s.STATE not in CLOSED and s not in OPEN then INSERT(s, OPEN)
11          else if s is in OPEN with a larger f then replace it with s
12  return FAILURE

    4.7 Greedy Best-First Search

    Definition. Greedy best-first search is best-first search with f(n) = h(n): it always expands the node that looks closest to the goal.

    f(n) = h(n)    where h(n) estimates the cost from n to the nearest goal
    • h(n) — heuristic function; a domain-specific estimate of remaining cost. h(goal) = 0.
    • Node selection — the frontier node of smallest h is expanded, regardless of how much has already been spent getting there.

    4.8 A* Search

    Definition. A* is best-first search with the evaluation function f(n) = g(n) + h(n): it expands the node with the lowest estimated cost of a complete solution through that node.

    f(n) = g(n) + h(n)
    • g(n) — the actual cost of the path from the start node to n (known exactly).
    • h(n) — the estimated cost from n to the nearest goal (an estimate).
    • f(n) — estimated total cost of the cheapest solution that passes through n.

    Combining both terms is what repairs greedy search: g remembers what has been spent, h anticipates what remains. Uniform-cost search is A* with h = 0; greedy search is A* with g = 0.

    A*(problem, h)
 1  n₀ ← ROOT ; g(n₀) ← 0 ; f(n₀) ← h(n₀)
 2  OPEN ← priority queue ordered by f, containing n₀ ;  CLOSED ← ∅
 3  while OPEN is not empty do
 4      n ← POP-MIN(OPEN)
 5      if GOAL-TEST(n) then return SOLUTION(n), cost g(n)
 6      add n to CLOSED
 7      for each successor s of n with step cost c(n, s) do
 8          g_new ← g(n) + c(n, s)
 9          if s in CLOSED and g_new ≥ g(s) then continue
10          if s not in OPEN or g_new < g(s) then
11              g(s) ← g_new ;  f(s) ← g(s) + h(s) ;  parent(s) ← n
12              insert or update s in OPEN
13  return FAILURE

    4.9 Adversarial search

    In a game the agent cannot assume its plan will be allowed to proceed: an opponent moves in between. Adversarial search computes a strategy — the best move now, assuming the opponent also plays the best reply. The two players are conventionally called MAX (the agent, trying to maximise the utility) and MIN (the opponent, trying to minimise it).

    4.9.1 The minimax procedure

    Definition. Minimax computes, for every node of the game tree, the utility of that position assuming both players play optimally: MAX nodes take the maximum of their children's values, MIN nodes take the minimum, and terminal nodes take their utility.

    MINIMAX(n) =
      UTILITY(n)  if n is terminal
      maxa MINIMAX(Result(n,a))  if PLAYER(n) = MAX
      mina MINIMAX(Result(n,a))  if PLAYER(n) = MIN 
    MINIMAX-DECISION(state)
 1  return the action a maximising MIN-VALUE(Result(state, a))

MAX-VALUE(state)
 1  if TERMINAL-TEST(state) then return UTILITY(state)
 2  v ← −∞
 3  for each a in ACTIONS(state) do v ← MAX(v, MIN-VALUE(Result(state, a)))
 4  return v

MIN-VALUE(state)
 1  if TERMINAL-TEST(state) then return UTILITY(state)
 2  v ← +∞
 3  for each a in ACTIONS(state) do v ← MIN(v, MAX-VALUE(Result(state, a)))
 4  return v

    4.9.2 Alpha-beta pruning

    Definition. Alpha-beta pruning is minimax with two running bounds that let whole subtrees be skipped when they cannot affect the root value. It returns exactly the same value as minimax while examining fewer nodes.

    ALPHA, BETA AND THE PRUNING CONDITION

    • α (alpha) — the best (highest) value MAX is currently guaranteed along the path to the current node. It starts at −∞ and only ever increases. Updated at MAX nodes.
    • β (beta) — the best (lowest) value MIN is currently guaranteed along the path. It starts at +∞ and only ever decreases. Updated at MIN nodes.
    • Pruning condition: stop examining the remaining children of a node as soon as α ≥ β. At a MAX node this is called a beta cut-off; at a MIN node an alpha cut-off.

    The intuition: if MIN already has a reply at least as good as something MAX can already guarantee elsewhere, MAX will never choose this branch, so the rest of it need not be examined.

    ALPHA-BETA-SEARCH(state)
 1  v ← MAX-VALUE(state, −∞, +∞) ; return the action of state whose value is v

MAX-VALUE(state, α, β)
 1  if TERMINAL-TEST(state) then return UTILITY(state)
 2  v ← −∞
 3  for each a in ACTIONS(state) do
 4      v ← MAX(v, MIN-VALUE(Result(state, a), α, β))
 5      if v ≥ β then return v            // beta cut-off: prune remaining children
 6      α ← MAX(α, v)
 7  return v

MIN-VALUE(state, α, β)
 1  if TERMINAL-TEST(state) then return UTILITY(state)
 2  v ← +∞
 3  for each a in ACTIONS(state) do
 4      v ← MIN(v, MAX-VALUE(Result(state, a), α, β))
 5      if v ≤ α then return v            // alpha cut-off: prune remaining children
 6      β ← MIN(β, v)
 7  return v

    COMMON MISTAKES — TOPIC 4

    • Wrong expansion order: not declaring whether children are pushed in forward or reverse order in DFS, producing a trace that contradicts the stated tree.
    • Confusing queue and stack: using a queue for DFS or a stack for BFS. BFS = FIFO, DFS = LIFO. Write the data structure at the top of your answer.
    • Reporting "failure" for a DLS cutoff. Cutoff and failure are different results.
    • Forgetting g(n) in A* and computing f = h. That turns A* into greedy search and loses every subsequent mark.
    • Returning the goal as soon as it is generated in A* instead of when it is expanded — this destroys optimality (in Worked Example 4.5 it would have returned cost 10).
    • Not updating g when a cheaper path to a node is found.
    • Claiming A* is always optimal without stating admissibility (tree search) or consistency (graph search).
    • Claiming BFS is always optimal — only with equal step costs.
    • Incorrect α/β updates: updating α at a MIN node or β at a MAX node. α belongs to MAX, β to MIN.
    • Pruning the node that triggered the cut-off instead of its unexamined siblings.
    • Applying min at a MAX level when backing values up the game tree.

    VSQA — 2 marks

    1. State the evaluation function of A* and define each term.2

    f(n) = g(n) + h(n), where g(n) is the actual cost from the start node to n and h(n) is the estimated cost from n to the goal; f(n) is the estimated cost of the cheapest solution through n.

    2. Which data structures do BFS and DFS use?2

    BFS uses a FIFO queue, so the shallowest node is expanded first. DFS uses a LIFO stack, so the deepest node is expanded first.

    3. What is an admissible heuristic?2

    One that never overestimates the true remaining cost: h(n) ≤ h*(n) for every node. Admissibility guarantees A*'s optimality in tree search.

    4. Define alpha and beta in alpha-beta pruning.2

    α is the highest value MAX is currently guaranteed along the path (starts at −∞, only increases); β is the lowest value MIN is currently guaranteed (starts at +∞, only decreases). Pruning occurs when α ≥ β.

    5. What does depth-limited search return when no goal is found within the limit?2

    CUTOFF — meaning no solution exists within depth ℓ but one may exist deeper. This is different from FAILURE, which means no solution exists at all in the explored space.

    6. Why is greedy best-first search not optimal?2

    Because f(n) = h(n) ignores g(n), the cost already incurred. It may therefore commit to a node that looks close to the goal but is reached or exited by expensive edges.

    7. State the time and space complexity of BFS.2

    Both are O(bd), where b is the branching factor and d the depth of the shallowest goal. The exponential space requirement is usually the binding limitation.

    5.2 Truth tables

    How to construct one.

    1. Count the distinct propositional variables, n. The table has 2n rows.
    2. List the variables as the first columns; fill them systematically (for two variables: TT, TF, FT, FF).
    3. Add one column for each sub-expression, working from the innermost brackets outward.
    4. Add the final column for the whole formula.
    5. Read the final column: all T = tautology; all F = contradiction; a mixture = contingency.

    Problem

    Construct the truth table of (p ∧ ¬q) → (p ∨ q) and classify it.

    Given

    Two variables p, q → 2² = 4 rows. Sub-expressions needed: ¬q, (p ∧ ¬q), (p ∨ q), then the implication.

    Method

    Evaluate innermost first, then combine.

    Step-by-step solution
    pq¬qp ∧ ¬qp ∨ q (p ∧ ¬q) → (p ∨ q)
    TTFFTT
    TFTTTT
    FTFFTT
    FFTFFT
    Result

    The final column is all T → the formula is a tautology.

    Explanation

    Rows 1, 3 and 4 are true because the antecedent (p ∧ ¬q) is false, and a false antecedent makes an implication true. Row 2 is the only row where the antecedent is true, and there the consequent (p ∨ q) is also true because p is true. Since whenever p ∧ ¬q holds p must hold, p ∨ q must hold too — so no falsifying row can exist.

    Exam tip

    Always include the intermediate columns. A correct final column with no working is usually worth half marks; the intermediate columns are where the rest are.

    5.4 Tautology, contradiction and contingency

    DEFINITIONS

    • Tautology (valid sentence): true under every interpretation — the final truth-table column is all T. Example: p ∨ ¬p.
    • Contradiction (unsatisfiable): false under every interpretation — all F. Example: p ∧ ¬p.
    • Contingency (satisfiable but not valid): true under some interpretations and false under others. Example: p ∧ q.

    Relationship: α is a tautology if and only if ¬α is a contradiction. This equivalence is the whole basis of the refutation method used later in this chapter.

    5.6 Propositional logic

    COMPONENTS

    • Proposition: a declarative statement that is either true or false, never both. "Kathmandu is in Nepal" is a proposition; "Close the door" is not.
    • Propositional symbols: p, q, r, … each standing for one atomic proposition, plus the constants True and False.
    • Connectives: ¬, ∧, ∨, →, ↔ as in §5.1.
    • Compound proposition: any well-formed combination of symbols and connectives, e.g. (p ∧ q) → ¬r.
    • Truth value / model: an assignment of T or F to every symbol. A sentence is satisfiable if some model makes it true, valid if every model does.
    • Entailment (KB ⊨ α): α is true in every model in which the knowledge base is true.

    Example. Let p = "It is raining", q = "The ground is wet", r = "The match is cancelled". Then:

    • p → q — if it is raining the ground is wet.
    • (p ∧ q) → r — if it is raining and the ground is wet, the match is cancelled.
    • Given p, we may conclude q by modus ponens, then r by modus ponens again.

    Limitation that motivates predicate logic: propositional logic has no internal structure. "All men are mortal" must be written as one indivisible symbol, so it cannot be connected to "Socrates is a man". To say something about every object of a kind, we need variables and quantifiers.

    5.8 Interpretation

    An interpretation in predicate logic gives meaning to the symbols. It consists of:

    1. a non-empty domain of discourse D — the set of objects being talked about;
    2. an assignment of one element of D to each constant;
    3. an assignment of a function Dn → D to each n-ary function symbol;
    4. an assignment of a relation over Dn (equivalently, the set of tuples for which it holds) to each n-ary predicate symbol.

    A sentence is true under an interpretation (the interpretation is a model of it) if the semantics evaluate it to true.

    5.9 Quantification

    THE TWO QUANTIFIERS

    • Universal ∀x P(x) — "for all x, P(x)". True when P holds of every object in the domain. Natural partner: →.
    • Existential ∃x P(x) — "there exists an x such that P(x)". True when P holds of at least one object. Natural partner: ∧.

    Negation equivalences (quantifier De Morgan): ¬∀x P(x) ≡ ∃x ¬P(x) and ¬∃x P(x) ≡ ∀x ¬P(x).

    Order matters when quantifiers are mixed: ∀x ∃y Loves(x, y) ("everyone loves someone, possibly a different person each") is not the same as ∃y ∀x Loves(x, y) ("there is one person whom everyone loves").

    5.10 Rules of inference

    An inference rule licenses deriving a new sentence from existing ones. A rule is sound if it never derives a false sentence from true premises — equivalently, if the corresponding implication is a tautology.

    RuleFormReads asWorked example
    Modus Ponensp → q,   p   ⊢   q If the implication holds and its antecedent holds, the consequent holds "If it rains the ground is wet"; "It rains" ⊢ "The ground is wet"

    And-eliminationp ∧ q   ⊢   p A conjunct may be taken alone"Raining and cold" ⊢ "Raining"
    And-introductionp,   q   ⊢   p ∧ q Separately true sentences may be joined"Raining", "Cold" ⊢ "Raining and cold"

    TWO FALLACIES TO AVOID

    • Affirming the consequent: from p → q and q, concluding p. Invalid — the ground may be wet because of a burst pipe.
    • Denying the antecedent: from p → q and ¬p, concluding ¬q. Also invalid.

    5.11 Forward chaining

    Definition. Forward chaining is data-driven inference: start from the known facts, fire every rule whose antecedent is satisfied, add the derived facts to the fact base, and repeat until the goal appears or no new fact can be derived.

    FORWARD-CHAIN(rules, facts, goal)
 1  repeat
 2      new ← ∅
 3      for each rule (A₁ ∧ … ∧ Aₙ → B) in rules do
 4          if every Aᵢ is in facts and B is not in facts then
 5              add B to new
 6              if B = goal then return TRUE with the derivation
 7      facts ← facts ∪ new
 8  until new = ∅
 9  return FALSE

    5.12 Backward chaining

    Definition. Backward chaining is goal-driven inference: start from the goal, find rules that could conclude it, and treat each antecedent of such a rule as a new subgoal. Recurse until every subgoal is a known fact (success) or no rule concludes it (failure).

    BACKWARD-CHAIN(goal, rules, facts)
 1  if goal ∈ facts then return TRUE
 2  for each rule (A₁ ∧ … ∧ Aₙ → goal) in rules do
 3      if for every i, BACKWARD-CHAIN(Aᵢ, rules, facts) = TRUE
 4          then return TRUE
 5  return FALSE                       // no rule proves the goal

    5.13 Unification

    Definition. Unification is the process of finding a substitution that makes two logical expressions syntactically identical. A substitution θ is a set of variable/term pairs written {x/a, y/f(b)}; applying it is written Expr·θ. Two expressions unify if such a θ exists, and the most general unifier (MGU) is the substitution that commits to as little as possible.

    UNIFICATION RULES

    1. Predicate symbols must be identical and have the same arity, otherwise failure.
    2. Compare arguments left to right.
    3. A variable unifies with any term (constant, variable or function term), giving a binding.
    4. Two different constants never unify.
    5. A variable may not be bound to two different terms within the same unification.
    6. Occurs check: a variable may not be bound to a term containing itself — e.g. x and f(x) do not unify.
    7. Apply each binding to the remaining arguments before continuing.

    WORKED EXAMPLE 5.6 — SIX UNIFICATION PROBLEMS

    Problem

    Find the MGU of each pair, or explain the failure.

    Step-by-step solutions
    (a)  P(x, f(y))  and  P(a, f(b))

    2ndf(y)f(b)same function symbol → compare arguments: y with b → bind y/b

    θ = {x/a, y/b}  →  unified result P(a, f(b)). The substitution works because after replacing x by a and y by b both expressions become literally the same string.

    (b)  Knows(John, x)  and  Knows(y, Mother(y))
    1st argumentJohn vs y → bind y/John
    Apply θ so farExpression 2 becomes Knows(John, Mother(John))

    1st argument: bind x/a. 2nd argument: x is now a, and a ≠ b. FAILURE — a variable cannot take two different values in one substitution.

    (d)  P(f(x), y)  and  P(y, a)

    Bind x/Ram, then y/z (or equivalently z/y). θ = {x/Ram, y/z} → Loves(Ram, z). This is the most general unifier: {x/Ram, y/Sita, z/Sita} also unifies them but commits unnecessarily to a particular value.

    Exam tip

    Write the substitution set explicitly, apply it to both expressions, and show that the results are identical. For a failure, name the rule violated (constant clash, double binding, or occurs check) — "they don't unify" alone does not earn the mark.

    5.14 Resolution and refutation (RRS)

    TERMINOLOGY — INCLUDING "RRS"

    • Literal: an atomic formula or its negation, e.g. P(x), ¬Q(a).
    • Clause: a disjunction of literals, e.g. ¬P(x) ∨ Q(x). The empty clause □ (also written NIL) is a clause with no literals; it is unsatisfiable and represents a contradiction.
    • CNF (conjunctive normal form): a conjunction of clauses. Every sentence has an equivalent CNF; resolution requires it.
    • Complementary literals: a pair such as P and ¬P (or, in FOPL, a pair that becomes complementary after unification).
    • Resolution: the inference rule that takes two clauses containing complementary literals and produces the resolvent — the disjunction of all their remaining literals, with the unifier applied.
    • Refutation: proving KB ⊨ α by showing that KB ∧ ¬α is unsatisfiable. Because α is entailed exactly when its negation is inconsistent with the KB, deriving □ proves α.
    • RRS — Resolution Refutation System. The syllabus abbreviation. It denotes the complete proof procedure: convert the knowledge base and the negated goal into clauses, then apply resolution repeatedly until the empty clause □ is derived. It is a refutation-complete proof system: if the clause set is unsatisfiable, resolution will eventually derive □.
    Resolution rule    (A ∨ P),  (¬P ∨ B)  ⊢  (A ∨ B)

    Converting to clause form (CNF)

    1. Eliminate ↔ : α ↔ β becomes (α → β) ∧ (β → α).
    2. Eliminate → : α → β becomes ¬α ∨ β.
    3. Move ¬ inwards using De Morgan and ¬∀x P ≡ ∃x ¬P, ¬∃x P ≡ ∀x ¬P.
    4. Standardise variables apart so no two quantifiers use the same variable name.
    5. Skolemise: replace each existentially quantified variable by a new constant if it is not inside any ∀, or by a Skolem function of the enclosing universal variables if it is. E.g. ∃x Rich(x) → Rich(K); ∀x ∃y Loves(x, y) → Loves(x, f(x)).
    6. Drop the universal quantifiers (remaining variables are implicitly universal).
    7. Distribute ∨ over ∧ to reach a conjunction of clauses, then write each clause separately.

    WORKED EXAMPLE 5.7 — PROPOSITIONAL RESOLUTION

    Problem

    Given P → Q, Q → R and P, prove R by resolution refutation.

    Given / method

    Convert to CNF, negate the goal, resolve until □.

    Step-by-step solution
    #ClauseSource

    5.15 Answer extraction from an RRS

    A refutation proves that something exists, but not which object. To recover the witness, add an answer literal: when negating a goal of the form ∃x G(x), write ¬G(x) ∨ Answer(x). Resolution then proceeds as usual, but instead of stopping at □, it terminates when the only literal remaining is the answer literal — whose arguments are the answer.

    WORKED EXAMPLE 5.9 — ANSWER EXTRACTION

    Problem

    Given "everyone who studies passes" and "Ram studies", find who passes.

    Given
    C1:  ¬Studies(x) ∨ Passes(x)          from ∀x (Studies(x) → Passes(x))
C2:  Studies(Ram)

C3:  ¬Passes(x) ∨ Answer(x)           negated query + answer literal

    Resolve as usual; read the answer off the final clause.

    Step-by-step solution
    StepClauses resolvedUnifierResolvent
    1C3 and C1 (on Passes){ } — same variable name x C4: ¬Studies(x) ∨ Answer(x)

    The derivation terminates with Answer(Ram), so the answer to "who passes?" is x = Ram.

    Explanation

    Without the answer literal, step 2 would have produced □ — a proof that someone passes, with the identity lost.     Exam tip

    State clearly that the answer literal is not part of the knowledge base; it is a bookkeeping device attached to the negated query.

    5.16 Rule-based deduction systems

    A rule-based deduction system represents knowledge as IF–THEN rules over facts and derives conclusions by applying those rules with an inference mechanism (forward or backward chaining). It is the deductive engine at the heart of expert systems.

    ComponentRole
    Facts (working memory)What is currently known about the case
    Rules (rule base)General domain knowledge as IF conditions THEN conclusion
    Inference mechanismMatches rules against facts and applies them — forward chaining (data-driven) or backward chaining (goal-driven), using unification when the rules contain variables
    ConclusionsNew facts derived, together with the chain of rules that produced them (the explanation)

    EXAMPLE — LOAN-APPROVAL DEDUCTION

    R1: IF income > 50000 AND employed        THEN good_income
R2: IF no_default AND credit_score > 700  THEN good_credit
R3: IF good_income AND good_credit        THEN eligible
R4: IF eligible AND collateral            THEN approve_loan

Facts: income = 60000, employed, no_default, credit_score = 750, collateral
    CycleRule firedBecauseDerived
    1R160000 > 50000 ✓, employed ✓good_income
    2R2no_default ✓, 750 > 700 ✓good_credit
    3R3good_income ✓, good_credit ✓eligible

    Explanation facility. Asked "why was the loan approved?", the system replays the chain: approve_loan ← R4 ← eligible ← R3 ← good_income (R1) and good_credit (R2). This traceability is the main practical advantage of rule-based deduction over a learned statistical model.

    COMMON MISTAKES — TOPIC 5

    • Incorrect truth-table rows: writing p → q as false when p is false. It is only false for T → F.
    • Wrong number of rows: 2n, not 2n.
    • Missing intermediate columns, so the working cannot be checked.
    • Pairing ∀ with ∧: ∀x (Student(x) ∧ Passed(x)) claims everything in the domain is a student. Use ∀ with → and ∃ with ∧.
    • Variable/constant confusion: writing Man(X) for a named individual, or using a capital for a variable inconsistently. Fix a convention and state it.
    • Invalid substitutions: binding one variable to two different terms, ignoring the occurs check, or failing to apply earlier bindings to the remaining arguments.
    • Forgetting to standardise variables apart before resolving two clauses.
    • Forgetting to negate the goal in a resolution proof — the commonest single error in this chapter. Without ¬goal, □ can never be derived.
    • Incorrect clause conversion: leaving an → inside a clause, or mishandling ¬ over a quantifier.
    • Skolemising an existential inside a ∀ with a constant instead of a function of the universal variable.
    • Stopping a resolution proof at the goal literal instead of continuing to □.
    • Confusing the direction of chaining: forward starts from facts, backward from the goal.

    6.1 Concepts of learning

    DEFINITION

    A program learns from experience E with respect to a task T and performance measure P if its performance at T, as measured by P, improves with E (Mitchell's formulation). Example: T = classifying e-mail as spam; P = classification accuracy; E = a corpus of messages labelled by users.

    Why learning is needed.

    • Designers cannot anticipate every situation the agent will face, so behaviour cannot be fully hand-coded.
    • Environments change; a learned model can be retrained where a hand-written rule must be rewritten.
    • For some tasks nobody can articulate the rule at all — recognising a handwritten digit is learned from examples, not from a definition of the digit "7".
    • Data often contains regularities too subtle or too numerous for a human to extract.

    The learning loop: experience/data → a learning algorithm → a hypothesis (the model) → applied to new inputs → performance measured → feedback improves the hypothesis.

    Type of feedbackLearning paradigmWhat the agent is given What it learns
    Correct answers suppliedSupervised learningInput–output pairs A function mapping input to output
    No answers suppliedUnsupervised learningInputs only Structure — clusters, groupings, associations
    Rewards and penaltiesReinforcement learningA reward signal after acting A policy: which action to take in which state

    6.2 Learning by analogy

    Concept. Learning by analogy transfers knowledge from a previously solved source problem to a structurally similar target problem, by mapping the correspondences between them.

    Process: (1) retrieve a similar past case; (2) map its components onto the new problem; (3) transfer the solution structure; (4) adapt it to the differences; (5) evaluate, and store the new case for future reuse.

    EXAMPLE

    A student who has learned to solve a problem on water flowing through pipes can solve a problem on current flowing through resistors by mapping pressure difference → voltage, flow rate → current, pipe narrowness → resistance. The equation flow = pressure/narrowness maps to I = V/R. The structure is transferred; the surface features (water, copper) are not.

    Practical AI use: case-based reasoning systems such as a help-desk adviser that retrieves the most similar previously resolved ticket and adapts its fix.

    6.3 Inductive learning

    Concept. Inductive learning generalises from specific observed examples to a general hypothesis. Given a training set of pairs (x, f(x)), it returns a hypothesis h that approximates the unknown target function f. Its conclusions are probable, not certain — a property called inductive bias.

    Process: observations → search a hypothesis space → select a hypothesis consistent with the examples (preferring simpler ones, by Occam's razor) → test it on unseen data.

    WORKED EXAMPLE 6.1 — INDUCTIVE GENERALISATION

    Problem

    From the examples below, induce a rule for when a customer receives a discount.

    ExampleMemberPurchase (₹)Discount?
    1Yes1200Yes
    2Yes800No
    3No1500No

    Step-by-step solution

    The threshold 1200 is not certain — the true boundary lies somewhere above 900 and at or below 1200. A different training set could move it. This uncertainty is inherent to induction: the hypothesis is supported by, not proved by, the data.

    6.4 Explanation-based learning (EBL)

    Concept. EBL learns a general rule from a single example by using existing domain knowledge to explain why that example is an instance of the concept, then generalising the explanation. It converts slow derivation into a fast rule — it is speed-up learning rather than the discovery of genuinely new knowledge.

    Inputs required: a training example; a goal concept; a domain theory (rules that can explain the example); and an operationality criterion (what the final rule is allowed to test).

    Process: (1) Explain — construct a proof that the example satisfies the goal concept using the domain theory. (2) Generalise — replace constants in the proof by variables, keeping only the conditions the proof actually used. (3) Refine — store the generalised rule for direct future use.

    WORKED EXAMPLE 6.2 — EBL

    Goal concept

    "x is a safe-to-stack object on y".

    Domain theory
    D1: lighter(x, y) → safe_to_stack(x, y)
D2: weight(x, w1) ∧ weight(y, w2) ∧ w1 < w2 → lighter(x, y)
D3: volume(x, v) ∧ density(x, d) ∧ w = v × d → weight(x, w)
    Training example

    Object book1: volume 0.1, density 0.4 → weight 0.04. Object table1: weight 30. Observed: stacking book1 on table1 is safe.

    Explanation (the proof)

    weight(book1) = 0.1 × 0.4 = 0.04 by D3; weight(table1) = 30; 0.04 < 30, so lighter(book1, table1) by D2; therefore safe_to_stack(book1, table1) by D1.

    Generalisation

    Replace the constants by variables and retain only the tested conditions:

    volume(x, v) ∧ density(x, d) ∧ weight(y, w2) ∧ (v × d) < w2  →  safe_to_stack(x, y)
    Result / explanation

    The colour of the book, the material of the table and every other unused property are dropped because the proof never referred to them. The new rule fires directly on any future pair of objects, so the multi-step derivation never has to be repeated.

    6.7 Naïve Bayes classifier

    SCIENTIFIC DESCRIPTION

    • Input: a labelled training set of discrete attribute vectors.
    • Assumption: the attributes are conditionally independent given the class. This is the "naïve" assumption — rarely exactly true, but the classifier is robust to violating it.
    • Model: the class prior P(H) and the conditional probabilities P(Eᵢ | H) for every attribute value and class, estimated from frequency counts.
    • Training: counting — no iteration, no optimisation.
    • Output: the class with the highest posterior probability.
    • Evaluation: accuracy/precision/recall on a held-out test set.
    P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E)
    TermNameMeaning
    P(H)Prior probabilityProbability of the class before seeing any evidence — its relative frequency in the training set
    P(E | H)LikelihoodProbability of observing the evidence given that the class holds
    P(E)Evidence / marginalOverall probability of the evidence; a normalising constant, identical for every class
    P(H | E)Posterior probabilityProbability of the class after seeing the evidence — what we want

    With the naïve independence assumption and evidence E = (e₁, e₂, …, eₙ):

    P(H | E) ∝ P(H) × P(e₁ | H) × P(e₂ | H) × … × P(eₙ | H)

    Because P(E) is the same for every class, the class with the largest numerator wins; dividing by P(E) is needed only when actual probabilities (not just the winner) are required.

    Problem

    Step 1 — priors. P(Yes) = 6/10 = 0.6; P(No) = 4/10 = 0.4.

    The prior favoured Yes (0.6 vs 0.4), but the evidence overturned it. The final 60/40 split is a moderate, not an overwhelming, prediction.

    THE ZERO-FREQUENCY PROBLEM AND LAPLACE SMOOTHING (supplementary)

    P(Overcast | No) = 0/4 = 0 in this dataset. Any day that is Overcast would therefore get a posterior of exactly 0 for No, no matter what the other attributes say — one missing count vetoes the whole product. Laplace (add-one) smoothing fixes this: add 1 to each count and k (the number of attribute values) to each denominator. Here P(Overcast | No) would become (0 + 1)/(4 + 3) = 1/7 ≈ 0.143.

    Exam tip

    Write every probability as a fraction with its count ("3 of 4") before converting to a decimal, and always finish with the comparison sentence and the named class. Check that your posteriors sum to 1 — it catches arithmetic slips instantly.

    Advantages and limitations of Naïve Bayes

    Advantages: extremely fast to train (counting only); works well with small datasets and high-dimensional inputs such as text; handles multi-class problems naturally; outputs calibrated-looking probabilities, not just a label.
    Limitations: the conditional-independence assumption is usually false, which distorts the probability values (though often not the ranking); the zero-frequency problem; and it cannot represent interactions between attributes.

    6.8 Decision Tree classifier

    SCIENTIFIC DESCRIPTION

    • Input: a labelled training set of attribute vectors.
    • Assumption: the class can be predicted by a sequence of tests on single attributes; short trees generalise better than long ones (Occam's razor).
    • Model: a tree whose internal nodes are attribute tests, branches are attribute values and leaves are class labels.
    • Training: recursive greedy splitting — at each node choose the attribute with the highest information gain (the ID3 algorithm).
    • Output: a class label, obtained by walking from the root to a leaf.
    • Evaluation: accuracy on a held-out test set; tree size is also reported, because a huge tree usually means overfitting.

    Structure of a decision tree

    PartMeaningIn the worked example
    Root nodeThe first attribute tested; the attribute that best separates the whole training setWeather
    Internal (decision) nodeA further attribute test applied to a subset of the dataTemperature, under the Sunny and Rainy branches
    BranchOne possible value of the attribute at the node above itSunny, Overcast, Rainy
    Leaf (terminal node)A class label — no further test is neededPlay = Yes, Play = No
    PathA root-to-leaf sequence; equivalent to one IF–THEN ruleIF Weather = Sunny AND Temperature = Cool THEN Play = Yes

    Entropy and information gain

    Entropy measures the impurity (disorder) of a set of examples with respect to the class label. It is 0 when every example has the same class and 1 (for two classes) when the classes are split exactly 50/50.

    Entropy(S) = − Σᵢ pᵢ log₂(pᵢ)

    where pᵢ is the proportion of examples in S belonging to class i, and the convention 0 · log₂0 = 0 is used.

    Information gain of attribute A is the reduction in entropy achieved by splitting S on A:

    Gain(S, A) = Entropy(S) − Σv ∈ Values(A) (|Sv| / |S|) · Entropy(Sv)

    The second term is the weighted average entropy of the children: each child's entropy is weighted by the fraction of examples that fall into it. The attribute with the largest gain is chosen for the node.

    USEFUL LOG VALUES FOR THE EXAM HALL

    log₂(1/2) = −1 · log₂(1/3) = −1.585 · log₂(2/3) = −0.585 · log₂(1/4) = −2 · log₂(3/4) = −0.415 · log₂(2/5) = −1.322 · log₂(3/5) = −0.737 · log₂(1/6) = −2.585 · log₂(5/6) = −0.263

    Two standard results worth memorising: a 1 : 1 split has entropy 1.000, a 1 : 2 split has entropy 0.9183, a 1 : 3 split has entropy 0.8113, and a pure node has entropy 0.

Being able to explain a disagreement like this is exactly what "applying a scientific approach to ML models" means.

    Advantages and limitations

    Advantages: highly interpretable — the tree is readable as rules; needs no data scaling or normalisation; handles categorical attributes and irrelevant attributes gracefully; classification is fast (one root-to-leaf walk).
    Limitations: greedy splitting can miss the globally best tree; strongly prone to overfitting on small or noisy datasets (this tree has six leaves for ten rows, which is a warning sign); information gain is biased toward attributes with many distinct values; small changes in the data can produce a completely different tree.

    BEYOND ID3 (supplementary)

    C4.5 uses gain ratio, which divides the gain by the attribute's own split information to remove the bias toward many-valued attributes; CART uses the Gini index Gini(S) = 1 − Σpᵢ² instead of entropy and builds binary trees. Pruning — removing subtrees that do not improve accuracy on validation data — is the standard cure for overfitting. The syllabus requires only ID3-style entropy and information gain.

    6.9 K-Means Clustering

    TERMINOLOGY NOTE

    The syllabus prints this topic as "K-Mean Cluttering algorithm". The standard academic name is K-Means Clustering — "means" because each cluster is represented by the mean (centroid) of its members, and "clustering" (not "cluttering") because the task is to group similar points. Use K-Means Clustering in your answers.

    SCIENTIFIC DESCRIPTION

    • Input: n unlabelled data points in a numeric space, and the number of clusters k.
    • Assumption: clusters are roughly spherical and of comparable size; distance is a meaningful measure of similarity; k is known in advance.
    • Model: k centroids; every point belongs to the cluster of its nearest centroid.
    • Training: iterative — assign points to nearest centroid, recompute centroids, repeat until assignments stop changing.
    • Output: k clusters and their final centroids.
    • Evaluation: the within-cluster sum of squared distances (SSE); the "elbow" of SSE plotted against k suggests a good k.
    TermMeaning
    ClusterA group of points that are more similar to each other than to points in other groups
    CentroidThe mean position of all points in a cluster — the coordinate-wise average
    kThe number of clusters, fixed by the user before the algorithm runs
    DistanceThe similarity measure; Euclidean distance is the standard choice
    Assignment stepEach point joins the cluster whose centroid is nearest
    Update stepEach centroid moves to the mean of the points now assigned to it
    ConvergenceThe algorithm stops when no point changes cluster (equivalently, the centroids stop moving)
    d(p, q) = √[ (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)² ]

    Euclidean distance between p = (x₁, y₁) and q = (x₂, y₂). The centroid of a cluster C = {p₁, …, pm} is

    centroid(C) = ( (1/m) Σ xᵢ , (1/m) Σ yᵢ )
    ALGORITHM K-Means(points P, integer k)
1. Choose k initial centroids C1, C2, ..., Ck
      (at random, or as k of the data points themselves)
2. repeat
3.     ASSIGNMENT STEP:
4.         for each point p in P do
5.             compute d(p, Cj) for every centroid Cj
6.             assign p to the cluster of the nearest centroid
7.     UPDATE STEP:
8.         for each cluster j do
9.             Cj <- mean of all points currently assigned to cluster j
10. until no point changed cluster        // convergence
11. return the k clusters and their centroids

    6.10 Q-Learning for learning policies

    SCIENTIFIC DESCRIPTION

    • Input: an environment the agent can act in and receive rewards from — no labelled examples at all.
    • Assumption: the environment behaves as a Markov decision process (the next state and reward depend only on the current state and action); every state–action pair is visited often enough.
    • Model: a Q-table storing Q(s, a), the expected long-term return of taking action a in state s and behaving well afterwards.
    • Training: repeated interaction; after every transition the Q-value of the state–action pair just used is updated toward the observed reward plus the discounted best next value.
    • Output: a policy π(s) = argmaxa Q(s, a) — the best action in each state.
    • Evaluation: total reward per episode; whether the learned policy reaches the goal reliably.

    Reinforcement learning in one paragraph

    Supervised learning is told the right answer; unsupervised learning is told nothing; reinforcement learning is told how good the outcome was. The agent observes a state, chooses an action, receives a numeric reward and lands in a new state. No one says which action was correct — the agent must work it out from delayed, noisy reward signals. This is the natural setting for game playing, robot control and any task where the correct move is only revealed by its consequences.

    TermSymbolMeaning
    StatesThe agent's current situation in the environment
    ActionaA choice available to the agent in state s
    RewardrThe immediate numeric feedback for taking a in s
    Next states′The state the agent lands in after the action
    Q-valueQ(s, a)Expected total discounted future reward of taking a in s — the "quality" of that action
    PolicyπThe rule mapping states to actions; the greedy policy takes the highest-Q action
    Learning rateαHow much of the new estimate replaces the old one; 0 ≤ α ≤ 1
    Discount factorγHow much future reward is worth relative to immediate reward; 0 ≤ γ ≤ 1

    Exploration vs exploitation. If the agent always takes the action with the current highest Q-value (exploitation), it may never discover a better one it has not tried. If it always acts randomly (exploration), it never uses what it has learned. The usual compromise is ε-greedy: with probability ε take a random action, otherwise take the best known action, and decrease ε as learning proceeds.

    Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α [ r + γ · maxa′ Q(s′, a′) − Q(s, a) ]
    PartNameRole
    Q(s, a)Old estimateWhat the agent believed before this experience
    αLearning rateα = 0 means never learn; α = 1 means throw away the old estimate entirely
    rImmediate rewardWhat the environment paid for this transition
    γDiscount factorγ near 0 → short-sighted; γ near 1 → far-sighted
    maxa′ Q(s′, a′)Best next valueThe best the agent believes it can do from the new state
    r + γ max Q(s′,a′)TargetThe improved estimate suggested by this experience
    [ target − Q(s,a) ]Temporal-difference errorHow wrong the old estimate was; the update moves α of the way toward fixing it

    Note that the max is over the actions available in s′, and it uses the agent's current Q-table — this is why Q-learning can learn the optimal policy even while behaving non-optimally (it is an off-policy method).

    ALGORITHM Q-Learning
1. initialise Q(s, a) <- 0 for all states s and actions a
2. for each episode do
3.     s <- initial state
4.     repeat
5.         choose action a from s using an exploration policy (e.g. epsilon-greedy on Q)
6.         take action a; observe reward r and next state s'
7.         Q(s, a) <- Q(s, a) + alpha * ( r + gamma * max_a' Q(s', a') - Q(s, a) )
8.         s <- s'
9.     until s is terminal
10. return policy pi(s) = argmax_a Q(s, a)

    WORKED EXAMPLE 6.6 — Q-LEARNING UPDATES

    Problem

    An agent in state s1 takes action a2, receives reward r = 10 and moves to state s2. The current Q-table holds Q(s1, a2) = 2, Q(s2, a1) = 5 and Q(s2, a2) = 8. With α = 0.5 and γ = 0.9, compute the updated Q(s1, a2). Then, from s2, the agent takes a2, receives r = 0 and reaches a state whose best Q-value is 4; compute the updated Q(s2, a2).

    Given

    α = 0.5 · γ = 0.9
    Update 1: s = s1, a = a2, r = 10, s′ = s2, Q(s1,a2) = 2, Q(s2,a1) = 5, Q(s2,a2) = 8
    Update 2: s = s2, a = a2, r = 0, maxa′ Q(s′,a′) = 4, Q(s2,a2) = 8 (before update 2)

    Method / Formula
    Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α [ r + γ · maxa′ Q(s′, a′) − Q(s, a) ]
    Update 1 — step by step

    Step 1 — best next value:
      maxa′ Q(s2, a′) = max(Q(s2,a1), Q(s2,a2)) = max(5, 8) = 8
    Step 2 — discount it:
      γ × 8 = 0.9 × 8 = 7.2
    Step 3 — form the target:
      r + γ max Q = 10 + 7.2 = 17.2
    Step 4 — temporal-difference error:
      17.2 − Q(s1,a2) = 17.2 − 2 = 15.2
    Step 5 — scale by α:
      0.5 × 15.2 = 7.6
    Step 6 — add to the old value:
      Q(s1, a2) = 2 + 7.6 = 9.6

    Update 2 — step by step

    maxa′ Q(s′, a′) = 4
    γ × 4 = 0.9 × 4 = 3.6
    target = r + 3.6 = 0 + 3.6 = 3.6
    TD error = 3.6 − 8 = −4.4
    α × (−4.4) = 0.5 × (−4.4) = −2.2
    Q(s2, a2) = 8 + (−2.2) = 5.8

    Result
    UpdateEntryOld QTarget TD errorNew Q
    1Q(s1, a2)2.017.2 +15.29.6
    2Q(s2, a2)8.03.6 −4.45.8
    Explanation

    Update 1 raises Q(s1, a2) sharply because the experience was much better than expected: a reward of 10 plus the promise of 8 more from s2 dwarfs the old estimate of 2. With α = 0.5 the agent moves only half of the way to the new target — this caution is what makes learning stable when rewards are noisy. Update 2 shows the other direction: the agent expected 8 from (s2, a2) but got no immediate reward and landed somewhere worth only 4, so the estimate is revised downward to 5.8. Q-values can fall as well as rise; they converge to the true expected return only after many visits.

    Exam tip

    Work strictly inside-out: first the max, then multiply by γ, then add r, then subtract the old Q, then multiply by α, then add. Writing those six lines separately protects the method marks. The two commonest slips are taking the max over the current state instead of the next state, and forgetting to subtract the old Q inside the bracket.

    WHY THERE IS A DISCOUNT FACTOR (supplementary)

    γ makes the infinite sum of future rewards r₀ + γr₁ + γ²r₂ + … finite whenever γ < 1, and it encodes a preference for sooner rewards. In a maze with a reward only at the exit, γ is what makes states near the exit acquire higher Q-values than distant ones — the reward "leaks backwards" one step per episode, which is why Q-learning needs many episodes to propagate value across a long path.

    6.11 Common mistakes — Topic 6

    COMMON MISTAKES

    1. Counting the class prior from the wrong column. P(Yes) is the proportion of rows with Play = Yes, not of any attribute value.
    2. Dividing the likelihood by the wrong total. P(Sunny | Yes) = (Sunny and Yes) ÷ (all Yes rows) = 1/6, not ÷ all Sunny rows.
    3. Forgetting the prior. Multiplying only the likelihoods and comparing — the prior P(H) must be one of the factors.
    4. Dividing by P(E) for one class only. Either divide both posteriors by P(E) or neither; comparing a normalised value against an unnormalised one is meaningless.
    5. Entropy sign errors. log₂ of a proportion is negative, so the minus sign in front of the sum makes the entropy positive. An entropy above 1 for a two-class problem is always wrong.
    6. Forgetting to weight the child entropies. Information gain subtracts the weighted average Σ(|Sᵥ|/|S|)·Entropy(Sᵥ), not the plain sum or plain average.
    7. Choosing the attribute with the lowest gain. The highest gain wins, because gain is the entropy removed.
    8. Not stopping at a pure node. Once a subset is all-Yes or all-No, it is a leaf — do not split it further.
    9. Forgetting the square root in K-Means. √(Δx² + Δy²), not Δx² + Δy². (The assignment often comes out the same, but the marks do not.)
    10. Updating centroids with old members. The new centroid is the mean of the points assigned in the current iteration.
    11. Computing the centroid as the middle of the range. It is the mean of the coordinates, not (min + max)/2.
    12. Declaring convergence too early. You must show one full iteration in which no point changes cluster.
    13. Taking the Q-learning max over the current state. The max is over the actions of the next state s′.
    14. Dropping the −Q(s, a) inside the bracket. Without it the update is not a temporal-difference correction and the value explodes.
    15. Confusing α and γ. α scales the whole correction; γ multiplies only the best next value.

    P(H | E) = P(E | H)·P(H) / P(E), where P(H) is the prior, P(E | H) the likelihood, P(E) the evidence (a normalising constant) and P(H | E) the posterior.

    3. Why is the Naïve Bayes classifier called "naïve"? 2

    Because it assumes all attributes are conditionally independent given the class, so their likelihoods can simply be multiplied. This assumption is rarely true in real data, but the classifier still ranks classes well.

    4. Write the entropy formula and state its value for a pure node. 2

    Entropy(S) = −Σ pᵢ log₂ pᵢ. For a pure node (all examples in one class) p = 1, log₂1 = 0, so Entropy = 0.

    5. Define information gain.2

    Gain(S, A) = Entropy(S) − Σᵥ (|Sᵥ|/|S|)·Entropy(Sᵥ): the reduction in entropy achieved by splitting S on attribute A. ID3 picks the attribute with the largest gain.

    6. Differentiate supervised and unsupervised learning. 2

    Supervised learning trains on labelled data and predicts a known output (Naïve Bayes, decision trees); unsupervised learning trains on unlabelled data and discovers structure such as clusters (K-Means).

    7. What is a centroid in K-Means?2

    The mean position of all points currently assigned to a cluster — computed coordinate by coordinate. It represents the cluster and is recomputed after every assignment step.

    8. State the Euclidean distance formula for two dimensions. 2

    d(p, q) = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²], the straight-line distance between p = (x₁, y₁) and q = (x₂, y₂).

    9. Write the Q-learning update rule.2

    Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α[r + γ·maxa′Q(s′, a′) − Q(s, a)], where α is the learning rate, γ the discount factor, r the immediate reward and s′ the next state.

    10. Distinguish exploration and exploitation.2

    Exploitation takes the action with the highest known Q-value to gain reward now; exploration takes a different action to gather information that may lead to higher reward later. ε-greedy balances the two.

    11. What is explanation-based learning?2

    Learning that uses domain knowledge to explain why a single training example is an instance of a concept, then generalises that explanation into a rule — so one example can be enough, unlike inductive learning.

    12. Define learning by analogy.2

    Solving a new problem by transferring the solution structure of a known, similar problem — mapping the corresponding parts of the two domains and adapting the solution.

    TOPIC 7Applications of Artificial Intelligence

    This chapter takes four concrete AI systems — the expert system, the neural network, the natural language processor and the machine-vision system — and shows, for each, what it is built from, how it works internally, and what it is used for. Two of them (expert systems and neural networks) are the direct engineering pay-off of Topics 5 and 6 respectively: an expert system is a rule-based deduction system with an interface around it, and a neural network is a learned function built from the weighted-sum unit.

    7.1 Expert systems

    DEFINITION

    An expert system is a computer program that uses knowledge captured from a human expert, together with an inference mechanism, to solve problems in a narrow domain at a level comparable to that expert — and that can explain the reasoning behind its conclusions.

    Purpose. Expert knowledge is scarce, expensive, slow to acquire and lost when the expert retires. An expert system makes it permanent, copyable, available around the clock and consistent — the same evidence always produces the same conclusion. It is used where the problem is knowledge-rich but narrow: the same reasoning applied over and over to different cases.

    Characteristics of an expert system

    CharacteristicExplanation
    High performance in a narrow domainExpert-level within its speciality, and useless outside it — there is no general common sense behind the rules
    Separation of knowledge and inferenceThe knowledge base and the inference engine are distinct modules, so knowledge can be edited without touching the program
    Symbolic reasoningIt manipulates facts and rules, not just numbers
    Explanation capabilityIt can answer "why do you ask?" and "how did you conclude that?" by reporting the rule chain
    Handling of uncertaintyMany systems attach certainty factors or probabilities to rules and conclusions
    Incremental growthNew rules can be added without redesigning the system
    Justifiable and auditableEvery conclusion traces back to named rules and facts — important in medicine, law and finance

    Applications

    Medical diagnosis and treatment advice; geological prospecting; equipment fault diagnosis and troubleshooting; computer system configuration; credit and loan assessment; tax and legal advice; process control and alarm interpretation; agricultural pest and crop advice. The classic historical examples are DENDRAL (inferring molecular structure), MYCIN (bacterial infection diagnosis with certainty factors) and XCON/R1 (configuring computer orders).

    Architecture of an expert system

    ComponentWhat it holds / doesWhy it matters
    Knowledge baseThe permanent domain knowledge: facts and IF–THEN production rules acquired from the expertThe system's expertise. Changing it changes the system's competence without any reprogramming
    Inference engineThe reasoning mechanism: matches rule conditions against working memory, resolves conflicts when several rules match, and fires the chosen rule. Uses forward chaining, backward chaining, or bothThe generic problem-solver — the same engine can drive a medical or a financial knowledge base

    WORKED EXAMPLE 7.1 — AN EXPERT SYSTEM CONSULTATION

    Problem

    A small expert system advises whether a crop field needs irrigation. Its knowledge base contains four rules. Trace one consultation and produce the explanation the system would give.

    Given — knowledge base

    Expert system development process

    #StageWhat happens
    1Problem identification & feasibilityCheck that the domain is narrow, that a genuine human expert exists, that the task is knowledge-intensive rather than computation-intensive, and that it takes the expert minutes-to-hours (not seconds, not weeks)
    2Knowledge acquisitionThe knowledge engineer elicits knowledge from the expert through interviews, observation of real cases and study of manuals. This is the hardest and slowest stage — the "knowledge-acquisition bottleneck"
    3Knowledge representationDecide the formalism: production rules, frames, semantic nets or logic; design the vocabulary of attributes and values
    4Rule development / encodingWrite the rules into the knowledge base and choose the inference strategy (forward chaining for monitoring and data-driven tasks, backward chaining for diagnosis and goal-driven tasks)
    5Prototype constructionBuild a small working system covering a slice of the domain, usually with an expert-system shell, to test the representation early
    6TestingRun stored cases with known answers; check the rule chains, not just the final answers
    7ValidationThe domain expert and end users confirm that the conclusions and the explanations are acceptable on fresh, real cases
    8DeploymentInstall for real users, integrate with existing systems, train the users
    9Maintenance & refinementAdd and correct rules as the domain changes; because knowledge is separate from the engine, this is editing, not reprogramming

    Identify → Acquire → Represent → Encode rules → Prototype → Test → Validate → Deploy → Maintain

    The process is iterative, not linear: testing and validation routinely send the team back to knowledge acquisition when a case exposes a missing or wrong rule.

    Advantages and limitations

    Advantages: preserves scarce expertise permanently; available everywhere, always, at low marginal cost; consistent — no fatigue and no mood; explains its reasoning; can be improved incrementally.
    Limitations: no common sense and no awareness of its own boundaries — it will apply its rules confidently to a case outside its domain; knowledge acquisition is slow and expensive; cannot learn from experience by itself (rules must be added by hand); struggles with genuinely novel situations and with uncertainty unless certainty factors are engineered in.

    7.2 Neural networks

    DEFINITION

    An artificial neural network is a computing system made of many simple interconnected processing units (neurons). Each unit computes a weighted sum of its inputs and passes it through an activation function; the network's behaviour is stored in the weights, which are learned from data rather than programmed.

    Mathematical model of a neuron

    A single artificial neuron receives inputs x₁, x₂, …, xₙ, each along a connection carrying a weight w₁, w₂, …, wₙ. It computes the weighted sum, adds a bias, and applies an activation function:

    net = Σᵢ wᵢxᵢ + b = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + wₙxₙ + b
    y = f(net)
    SymbolNameMeaning
    xᵢInputThe i-th signal entering the neuron — a feature value or the output of a previous neuron
    wᵢWeightThe strength of connection i. Positive = excitatory, negative = inhibitory, zero = no influence. This is what learning changes
    bBiasA constant added to the sum; it shifts the decision boundary so the neuron does not have to pass through the origin. Equivalent to a weight w₀ on a permanently-1 input
    netNet inputThe total stimulation reaching the neuron
    fActivation functionDecides the output from the net input; adds the non-linearity that makes multi-layer networks powerful
    yOutputWhat the neuron sends onward

    Activation functions

    FunctionDefinitionOutput rangeTypical use
    Step / thresholdf(net) = 1 if net ≥ 0, else 0{0, 1} Classic perceptron; logic-gate realisation (used below)
    Signf(net) = +1 if net ≥ 0, else −1{−1, +1} Bipolar perceptrons
    Sigmoidf(net) = 1 / (1 + e−net)(0, 1) Differentiable, so it permits gradient-based training; probability-like output
    ReLUf(net) = max(0, net)[0, ∞) Standard in deep networks — cheap and avoids vanishing gradients (supplementary)
    Linearf(net) = net(−∞, ∞) Output units in regression tasks

    For all the gate calculations below the step function is used, with the threshold written into the bias: y = 1 if net ≥ 0, otherwise y = 0. Writing the threshold θ as a bias b = −θ is standard, and it lets one uniform rule handle every gate.

    7.3 Gate realization

    A single neuron with a step activation is a linear threshold unit: it outputs 1 on one side of the line w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b = 0 and 0 on the other. Any Boolean function whose true and false input points can be separated by a straight line — a linearly separable function — can therefore be realised by one neuron. AND, OR, NOT and NAND are all linearly separable; XOR is not.

    Problem

    Show that no single neuron with a step activation can realise XOR, and give a network that can.

    7.4 Network structure

    Single neurons are combined into layers. In the standard feed-forward (multi-layer perceptron) architecture every neuron in one layer connects to every neuron in the next, and signals travel in one direction only — from input to output, with no cycles.

    LayerRoleSize determined by
    Input layerReceives the raw feature values and passes them on; these units do no computationThe number of input features
    Hidden layer(s)Compute intermediate representations; each neuron performs net = Σwx + b followed by an activation. This is where non-linear combinations of features are formed — the layer that makes XOR possibleChosen by the designer; too few underfits, too many overfits
    Output layerProduces the final result — one unit for a binary decision or a regression value, one unit per class for multi-class classificationThe number of outputs required by the task

    Feed-forward flow

    One forward pass proceeds layer by layer:

    1. present the input vector x to the input layer
2. for each hidden neuron j:
       net_j = sum_i (w_ij * x_i) + b_j
       h_j   = f(net_j)
3. for each output neuron k:
       net_k = sum_j (v_jk * h_j) + b_k
       y_k   = f(net_k)
4. output the vector y

    Every neuron in a layer can be computed independently and in parallel, which is why networks map so well onto GPUs. Learning (training) is the separate process of adjusting the weights so that the outputs match the targets; the standard method is backpropagation, which propagates the output error backwards through the layers and adjusts each weight in proportion to its contribution to that error. (The syllabus requires the mathematical model, gate realization and network structure; backpropagation is mentioned here only to complete the picture — supplementary.)

    7.5 Natural Language Processing

    DEFINITION

    Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the branch of AI concerned with enabling computers to understand, interpret and generate human language — written or spoken — in a way that is useful.

    Purpose. Most human knowledge is recorded in natural language, and natural language is the interface people find easiest. NLP lets a system read that knowledge and lets users talk to it without learning a formal query language. Why it is hard: natural language is ambiguous at every level — lexical ("bank" = riverside or financial institution), syntactic ("I saw the man with the telescope" — who has the telescope?), semantic and referential ("she told her that she had won" — who won?) — and much of the meaning depends on context that is never stated.

    Steps of NLP parsing

    Understanding proceeds through a pipeline of levels, each taking the output of the previous one and adding a layer of structure.

    #StepWhat it doesOutput
    1Lexical / morphological analysisSplits the input into tokens (words and punctuation), analyses word structure — stems, prefixes, suffixes, inflections — and looks each word up in the lexicon to find its possible parts of speechA list of tokens, each with its root form and candidate word classes
    2Syntactic analysis (parsing)Applies the grammar to arrange the tokens into a valid structure; rejects ungrammatical sequences; resolves part-of-speech ambiguity by choosing a structure the grammar permitsA parse tree showing noun phrase, verb phrase and their constituents
    3Semantic analysisAssigns meaning to the structure: maps words to concepts, assigns roles (who did what to whom), and rejects structurally valid but meaningless readingsA meaning representation — for instance a logical form or a set of predicates
    4Discourse integrationLinks the sentence to the sentences around it: resolves pronouns and references, tracks the entities already introducedA meaning in which references are bound to actual entities
    5Pragmatic analysisInterprets the utterance in its context of use — the speaker's intention, indirect requests, world knowledgeThe intended meaning and the action the system should take

    Step 3 — semantic analysis

    eat(agent = boy, object = apple), tense = present

    The verb "eat" requires an animate agent and an edible object; boy and apple satisfy both restrictions, so the reading is accepted. A sentence such as "The apple eats the boy" would parse correctly at step 2 but fail these selectional restrictions at step 3 — which is precisely why the two steps are separate.

    Step 4 — discourse integration

    "The boy" uses the definite article, so it refers to a boy already introduced in the preceding discourse; the system binds it to that entity rather than creating a new one. "An apple" is indefinite, so a new entity is introduced. If the next sentence were "He liked it", the pronouns would be resolved against these two entities.

    Step 5 — pragmatic analysis

    In context the utterance is a statement of fact, so the appropriate system response is to record it. The same words could be a warning ("the boy eats an apple" said to someone with a nut allergy present) — context, not syntax, decides.

    Result

    Meaning representation eat(boy₁, apple₁), present tense, asserted as a fact about an already-known boy.

    Exam tip

    Name the five steps in order, give one line of what each produces, then carry one short sentence all the way through — the parse tree is the part most often worth full marks, so draw it even if the question does not explicitly ask for it.

    Applications of NLP

    Machine translation; question answering and chatbots; information retrieval and search; text summarisation; sentiment analysis; spell and grammar checking; speech recognition and synthesis; named-entity extraction from documents.

    7.6 Basic concepts of machine vision

    DEFINITION

    Machine vision (computer vision) is the branch of AI that enables a machine to acquire images of the real world and extract useful information from them — identifying objects, their positions, their properties and their relationships — so that the system can describe or act upon what it sees.

    An image arrives as nothing but a grid of intensity numbers. The difficulty of vision is that the mapping from a three-dimensional scene to that grid destroys information: the same object produces completely different numbers under different lighting, at a different angle, at a different distance or when partly hidden. Vision is therefore an inference problem — reconstructing a plausible scene from insufficient data — and not an image-processing problem.

    The machine-vision pipeline

    #StageWhat happens
    1Image acquisitionA camera or other sensor captures the scene and digitises it into a pixel array. Lighting, resolution, focus and camera placement are engineering decisions that determine how hard every later stage will be
    2Pre-processingImproves image quality for analysis: noise removal by smoothing/filtering, contrast enhancement, grey-scale conversion, geometric correction, edge sharpening
    3SegmentationDivides the image into meaningful regions — separating objects from background — using thresholding, edge detection or region growing. This answers "where are the things?"
    4Feature extractionMeasures properties of each region that are useful for identification: area, perimeter, centroid, aspect ratio, edges, corners, texture, colour histograms, shape descriptors
    5Recognition / classificationMatches the feature vector against known models or feeds it to a trained classifier (neural network, decision tree, nearest neighbour) to name the object
    6Interpretation / decisionBuilds a description of the whole scene — the objects, their relationships and their positions — and issues the action the application requires (accept/reject the part, steer the vehicle, open the gate)

    Applications of machine vision

    Industrial inspection and quality control on production lines; robot guidance and pick-and-place; optical character recognition and document digitisation; face and fingerprint recognition; medical image analysis (X-ray, MRI, retinal scans); autonomous vehicles (lane, sign, pedestrian and obstacle detection); agricultural crop and fruit grading; surveillance and traffic monitoring; barcode and number-plate reading.

    7.7 Common mistakes — Topic 7

    COMMON MISTAKES

    1. Confusing the knowledge base with working memory. The knowledge base holds permanent rules; working memory holds facts about the current case and is cleared afterwards.
    2. Omitting the explanation facility from the expert-system architecture diagram — it is a named component in the syllabus and usually carries a mark.
    3. Saying an expert system "learns". A standard expert system does not; its rules are added by a knowledge engineer.
    4. Presenting the development process as strictly linear. Testing and validation routinely send the team back to knowledge acquisition.
    5. Forgetting the bias in the weighted sum. net = Σwᵢxᵢ + b; dropping b makes AND and OR indistinguishable.
    6. Getting the sign of the bias wrong. A threshold θ becomes a bias b = −θ. AND has b = −1.5, not +1.5.
    7. Using the wrong comparison in the step function. Fix one convention (y = 1 if net ≥ 0) and apply it to every row; AND with b = −1.5 relies on 0.5 ≥ 0.
    8. Not computing every input combination. A gate realisation is only proved when all 2ⁿ rows are shown.
    9. Claiming a single neuron can realise XOR. It cannot — show the contradiction from the four inequalities.
    10. Drawing the input layer as if it computes. Input units only distribute values; the first weighted sum happens at the hidden layer.
    11. Listing the NLP steps out of order or merging discourse with semantics. Lexical → syntactic → semantic → discourse → pragmatic.
    12. Confusing syntactic and semantic failure. "The apple eats the boy" is syntactically fine and semantically anomalous — that distinction is the point of the example.
    13. Treating machine vision as image processing. Pre-processing is one stage of six; vision ends in recognition and interpretation.
    14. Swapping segmentation and feature extraction. Segmentation finds the regions; feature extraction measures them.

    It shifts the decision boundary by setting how large the weighted sum must be before the neuron fires; a threshold θ is written as a bias b = −θ.

    Because XOR is not linearly separable — no single straight line separates {(0,1), (1,0)} from {(0,0), (1,1)}. A hidden layer is required.

    Input layer (distributes feature values), hidden layer(s) (compute intermediate representations), output layer (produces the result). Signals flow forward only.

    PRACTICE 3.4 — Q-LEARNING UPDATES WITH NEW VALUES

    Problem

    α = 0.4, γ = 0.8. The agent is in state s3, takes action a1, receives r = 5 and moves to s4, where Q(s4, a1) = 3, Q(s4, a2) = 10 and Q(s4, a3) = 7. Currently Q(s3, a1) = 6. Compute the updated Q(s3, a1). Then compute a second update in which the agent, from s4, takes a2, receives r = −2 and reaches a state whose best Q-value is 1.

    Update 1

    max Q(s4, ·) = max(3, 10, 7) = 10
    γ × 10 = 0.8 × 10 = 8
    target = r + 8 = 5 + 8 = 13
    TD error = 13 − 6 = 7
    α × 7 = 0.4 × 7 = 2.8
    Q(s3, a1) = 6 + 2.8 = 8.8

    Update 2

    max Q(next) = 1
    γ × 1 = 0.8
    target = −2 + 0.8 = −1.2
    TD error = −1.2 − 10 = −11.2
    α × (−11.2) = 0.4 × (−11.2) = −4.48
    Q(s4, a2) = 10 + (−4.48) = 5.52

    Result

    Q(s3, a1) rises from 6 to 8.8; Q(s4, a2) falls from 10 to 5.52.

    Explanation

    Update 2 shows a negative reward and a poor successor combining to produce a large negative TD error. Because α = 0.4, only 40% of that correction is applied — a large single experience never wipes out an estimate built from many. Notice also that update 1 used Q(s4, a2) = 10 before update 2 reduced it; in real training the table keeps shifting, which is why convergence needs many visits.

    f(n) = g(n) + h(n)   (A* evaluation function)
    SymbolMeaning
    g(n)Actual cost of the path found so far from the start node to n
    h(n)Heuristic estimate of the cheapest cost from n to a goal

    Special cases: h(n) = 0 for all n gives uniform-cost search; g(n) = 0 gives greedy best-first search.

    Admissible: h(n) ≤ h*(n) for every n
    Consistent: h(n) ≤ c(n, n′) + h(n′) for every edge n → n′, and h(goal) = 0

    R1 — Topic 1: Introduction to AI

    AI = building systems that think or act humanly or rationally; the modern engineering standard is the rational agent. History markers: McCulloch–Pitts neuron 1943; Turing's test 1950; the Dartmouth workshop of 1956 names the field; early optimism; the AI winters of funding collapse; expert systems commercialise AI in the 1980s; Deep Blue beats Kasparov in 1997; statistical and then deep learning dominate from the 2000s as data and compute arrive. Strong AI claims a machine can genuinely have a mind; weak AI claims only that it can act as if it does — all working systems today are weak AI. Characteristics: reasoning, learning, perception, knowledge representation, problem solving, language use, planning, adaptation, handling uncertainty. AI vs conventional computing: symbolic and heuristic vs algorithmic and numeric; satisfactory vs guaranteed-correct answers; explanation vs none.

    R2 — Topic 2: Intelligent Agent

    An agent perceives through sensors and acts through actuators; the agent function f : P* → A maps every percept sequence to an action. A rational agent selects the action expected to maximise its performance measure, given the percept sequence and its built-in knowledge — rational is not the same as omniscient or perfect. PEAS = Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors. Environment properties: fully vs partially observable, deterministic vs stochastic, episodic vs sequential, static vs dynamic, discrete vs continuous, single vs multi-agent, known vs unknown. Sensor perceives, effector is the physical part that moves, actuator drives the effector, action is the choice itself.

    R3 — Topic 3: Problem Solving

    Five components of a well-defined problem: initial state, actions/operators, transition model, goal test, path cost. The state space is the set of all reachable states. Water jug (4 L, 3 L → 2 L): six steps, (0,0) → (4,0) → (1,3) → (1,0) → (0,1) → (4,1) → (2,3). 8-puzzle: 9!/2 = 181,440 reachable states — half of all arrangements are unreachable because a legal move never changes the parity. CSP = variables + domains + constraints; solved by backtracking, improved by MRV, degree heuristic, LCV and forward checking. Production system = global database (working memory) + production rules + control strategy, driven by the recognise–act cycle; the animal example derives has_hair → mammal → eats_meat → carnivore → tawny + spots → cheetah.

    R4 — Topic 4: Searching Techniques

    Uninformed search has no domain information: DFS (stack, low memory, not complete, not optimal), BFS (queue, complete, optimal with equal step costs, memory-hungry), DLS (DFS with a limit; returns CUTOFF when d > ℓ). Informed search uses h: greedy expands the node with the smallest h alone (fast, not optimal — on the guide's graph it returns S → A → G at cost 10); A* uses f = g + h and returns the optimal S → B → G at cost 7, expanding S, A, B, G and never touching C. Admissible: never overestimates → A* is optimal. Adversarial: minimax backs values up from the leaves (root value 5, move to B in Fig 4.3); alpha–beta returns the same value while pruning any branch where α ≥ β — it examined 5 of the 8 leaves there.

    R5 — Topic 5: Knowledge Representation

    Connectives: ¬, ∧, ∨, →, ↔ with precedence ¬ > ∧ > ∨ > → > ↔. A formula is a tautology if true in every row, a contradiction if false in every row, a contingency otherwise. Propositional logic cannot generalise over objects; FOPL adds constants, variables, predicates, functions and quantifiers. ∀ pairs with →, ∃ pairs with ∧. Forward chaining is data-driven, backward chaining goal-driven. Unification finds the MGU; it fails on a variable bound to two different constants, a constant against a function, or the occurs check. Resolution/RRS: convert to CNF, negate the goal, resolve until the empty clause □ appears; adding an answer literal turns the prover into a question-answering system.

    R6 — Topic 6: Machine Learning

    Learning = improving at task T on measure P with experience E. Styles: analogy (map a known solution onto a new problem), inductive (generalise from many examples), explanation-based (generalise one example using a domain theory). Feedback types: supervised (labels), unsupervised (none), reinforcement (reward). On the shared 10-row dataset (6 Yes, 4 No): Naïve Bayes classifies (Sunny, Cool) as No with posterior 0.6; ID3 gives Entropy(S) = 0.9710 and gains 0.3219 (Weather), 0.0955 (Temperature), 0.0200 (Windy), so Weather is the root and Overcast is a pure leaf — and the resulting tree classifies that same instance as Yes, a genuine disagreement caused by the independence assumption.     R7 — Topic 7: Applications of AI

    Expert system components: knowledge base (permanent rules), inference engine (match–select–fire), working memory (case facts), user interface, explanation facility, knowledge acquisition. Development: identify → acquire → represent → encode → prototype → test → validate → deploy → maintain, iteratively; knowledge acquisition is the bottleneck.     Medical image screening — machine vision with a trained classifier. Spam filtering — Naïve Bayes classification over word features.

    3. Distinguish AI, machine learning and deep learning. 2

    AI is the whole field of building intelligent systems; machine learning is the subset that improves from data; deep learning is the subset of machine learning using multi-layer neural networks.

    Topic 2

    4. What is an agent function?2

    A mapping f : P* → A from every possible percept sequence to an action. The agent program is the concrete implementation of that function.

    5. Define rationality for an agent.2

    Selecting, for each percept sequence, the action expected to maximise the performance measure given the evidence from the percept sequence and the agent's built-in knowledge. Rationality is not omniscience — an agent can be rational and still get a bad outcome.

    6. Differentiate an actuator from an effector.2

    An effector is the physical part that acts on the environment (a wheel, a gripper); an actuator is the mechanism that drives the effector (a motor, a hydraulic cylinder).

    7. What is a deterministic environment?2

    One in which the next state is completely determined by the current state and the agent's action, with no randomness. Chess is deterministic; driving is stochastic.

    Topic 3

    8. What is a state space?2

    The set of all states reachable from the initial state by any sequence of legal actions, together with the transitions between them. Searching means exploring this space for a path to a goal state.

    9. List the components of a well-defined problem.2

    Initial state, set of actions/operators, transition model, goal test and path-cost function.

    10. Define a constraint satisfaction problem.2

    A problem given as a set of variables, a domain of values for each, and constraints restricting which combinations are allowed. A solution assigns a value to every variable so that all constraints hold.

    11. What are the components of a production system?2

    A global database (working memory), a set of production rules, and a control strategy that runs the recognise–act cycle of matching, conflict resolution and firing.

    Topic 4

    12. Compare BFS and DFS in one line each.2

    BFS uses a queue, is complete and optimal with equal step costs but needs O(bd) memory. DFS uses a stack and needs only O(bm) memory but is neither complete nor optimal.

    13. What does depth-limited search return when the goal is deeper than the limit?2

    CUTOFF — meaning "no solution was found within this limit", which is different from FAILURE, meaning "no solution exists at all".

    14. Define an admissible heuristic.2

    One that never overestimates: h(n) ≤ h*(n) for every node n, where h* is the true cheapest cost to a goal. Admissibility is what makes A* optimal in tree search.

    15. Why is greedy best-first search not optimal?2

    Because it ignores g(n), the cost already paid. It commits to whatever looks closest to the goal, so a cheap-looking first step can lead onto an expensive overall path.

    16. State the alpha–beta pruning condition.2

    Prune the remaining children of a node when α ≥ β, where α is the best value MAX can already guarantee and β the best MIN can already guarantee, on the path to the root.

    Topic 5

    17. Define a tautology.2

    A formula that is true under every possible assignment of truth values — every row of its truth table is T. Example: p ∨ ¬p.

    18. Give one limitation of propositional logic that FOPL removes. 2

    Propositional logic cannot generalise over objects — "all students pass" needs one proposition per student. FOPL adds variables, predicates and quantifiers, so ∀x (Student(x) → Passes(x)) covers them all.

    19. What is a most general unifier?2

    The substitution that makes two expressions identical while committing to as little as possible, so that every other unifier is an instance of it.

    20. State the refutation principle.2

    KB ⊨ α if and only if KB ∧ ¬α is unsatisfiable. Resolution proves α by deriving the empty clause from the knowledge base together with the negated goal.

    21. What is the occurs check?2

    The test that prevents binding a variable to a term containing that same variable — for instance x with f(x) — which would create an infinite term. Ignoring it makes unification unsound.

    22. Name two approaches to knowledge representation.2

    Logical representation (FOPL sentences with formal inference) and production rules (IF–THEN rules fired by an inference engine). Semantic networks and frames are two further approaches.

    Topic 6

    23. Why does Naïve Bayes need smoothing?2

    Because a zero count makes one likelihood 0, and the product of the likelihoods is then 0 regardless of the other evidence. Laplace smoothing adds 1 to each count to prevent this.

    24. What is the entropy of a set that is half Yes and half No? 2

    Exactly 1.000 bit — the maximum for two classes: −0.5 log₂0.5 − 0.5 log₂0.5 = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.

    25. When does K-Means stop?2

    When no point changes cluster between two successive assignment steps — equivalently, when the centroids no longer move.

    26. What does the discount factor γ do in Q-learning? 2

    It sets how much future reward counts relative to immediate reward. γ near 0 makes the agent short-sighted; γ near 1 makes it value long-term return, and keeps the sum of future rewards finite.

    27. Give one advantage of a decision tree over Naïve Bayes. 2

    It is directly interpretable — each root-to-leaf path reads as an IF–THEN rule — and it can represent interactions between attributes, which the independence assumption forbids.

    Topic 7

    28. Why is the knowledge base kept separate from the inference engine? 2

    So domain knowledge can be edited, corrected or replaced without changing the program, and so the same engine can drive knowledge bases for different domains.

    29. Give the weights and bias for a NOR gate.2

    w₁ = −1, w₂ = −1, b = 0.5 with the step activation — the OR parameters with every sign reversed.

    30. What does segmentation do in machine vision?2

    It divides the pre-processed image into meaningful regions, separating objects from the background, so that features can then be measured for each region.

    31. What is pragmatic analysis in NLP?2

    Interpreting an utterance in its context of use — the speaker's intention and relevant world knowledge — so that, for example, "can you pass the salt?" is treated as a request rather than a question about ability.
